Dilemma 1 of 6 · 1968

1968: Join the New Left or Join the Counterculture?

You are there

It is 1968. You are a college student, and you are against the Vietnam War. You are not alone. Millions of young men your age face the draft. Many young people feel let down by the government and by the way things are.

Two groups are growing. Both reject the way things are now. The first is the New Left. It began in 1960 as Students for a Democratic Society (SDS). One of its founders, Tom Hayden, wrote that young people should stop only reading history and start making history. Their goal is to make the United States more democratic. SDS chapters now meet on more than 1,000 campuses.

The second group is the counterculture. Its members are often called hippies. Many care less about elections and more about how they live. They want to explore who they are. Their motto is “Make Love, Not War.”

What really happened

Both movements grew, and Norton says they overlapped. But they were not the same. The New Left was the political side of the youth revolt. SDS grew to more than 1,000 campuses and printed its own underground newspaper, called The Rag. As the draft reached more young men, many students turned to anti-war work. About 200,000 young men ignored their draft notices, and about 4,000 went to prison for it. Another 56,000 qualified as conscientious objectors. That means they showed strong moral, ethical, or religious opposition to war.

The counterculture was different. Norton calls it an unorganized and leaderless rebellion. Tom Hayden later said most young rebels were not mainly interested in winning office. They cared more about changing how they lived. Norton says most hippies chose to “drop out” of mainstream society instead of trying to change the political system. They believed change would grow from the alternative communities they built.

Norton says the counterculture was mostly middle-class White young people. They were upset about the Vietnam War, racism, political corruption, and parental authority.

Source: Norton Ch. 28, “The New Left,” pp. 1260–1261; “Anti-War Protests,” pp. 1262–1263; “The Counterculture,” pp. 1265 and 1268.

Dilemma 2 of 6 · 1973

1973: Vote to Ratify the Equal Rights Amendment or Vote No?

You are there

It is 1973. You are a state legislator. In 1972, Congress approved an equal-rights amendment (ERA) to the U.S. Constitution. Now each state must decide whether to ratify it. It needs thirty-eight states. By mid-1973, twenty-eight states have said yes.

The amendment is short. It says: “Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex.” If ratified, Norton says it would require equal treatment for women throughout society and politics.

Supporters include Gloria Steinem and the National Organization for Women (NOW). NOW was founded in 1966. It works to end gender discrimination in the workplace. Norton says the civil rights movement inspired many women to demand equal rights.

Opponents are conservative groups. One is led by Phyllis Schlafly. It is called STOP ERA. STOP stands for “Stop Taking Our Privileges.” Their signs say “Stop E R A” and “E R A is a Crushing Blow to American Families.”

What really happened

Legislators in different states made different choices. Norton says conservative groups led by Phyllis Schlafly’s STOP ERA organization stopped ratification in several state legislatures. By 1982, the ERA had died. It was three states short of the thirty-eight it needed.

Norton says the loss of the ERA did not end the push for women’s rights. Another law passed in 1972 had far-reaching effects. Title IX of the Educational Amendments of 1972 bars gender discrimination in any education program that gets federal money. It opened doors for women in higher education, including admissions, teaching jobs, and classes.

Norton also says conservatives gained strength from their win over the ERA. They drew on a growing backlash against “radical feminism.”

Late 1969: Fight the New Environmental Laws or Sign Them?

You are there

It is late 1969. You are an adviser to President Richard Nixon. Nixon won the 1968 election with votes from people tired of student protests and the changes of the 1960s. In his first term, he plans to focus on programs that please conservatives and help him win a second term.

Then two disasters make the news. Off the coast of Santa Barbara, California, an oil spill covers 200 miles of beaches. It kills thousands of sea birds and marine animals. Six months later, in June 1969, the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland, Ohio, catches fire. It burns for five days.

Many Americans now want more protection for the environment. A New York Times survey of college campuses shows many young people moving from anti-war work to environmental causes. Democrats hold the majority in both the House and the Senate, and Congress is working on environmental laws.

What really happened

Nixon signed them. Norton says he knew that if he vetoed the bills, the Democratic majorities in Congress would overrule him. So he chose not to stand in the way. In late 1969, he grudgingly signed the Endangered Species Preservation Act and the National Environmental Policy Act. In 1970, he signed the Clean Air Act. He also created two federal agencies by executive order: the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA).

But Nixon did not always go along. In 1972, he vetoed a new Clean Water Act, and Congress overrode his veto. Norton also says he weakened many new environmental laws by refusing to spend the money Congress set aside for them.

Dilemma 4 of 6 · 1969

1969: Withdraw Step by Step or Bring Troops Home Much Faster?

You are there

It is 1969. You are an adviser to President Richard Nixon. The war in Vietnam is unpopular and costly. About 560,000 U.S. troops are in Vietnam. That is the highest number of the whole war.

Peace talks in Paris are stuck. The United States wants Viet Cong forces to leave South Vietnam and wants to keep the South Vietnamese government of President Nguyen Van Thieu. The North Vietnamese and Viet Cong want to keep Communist forces in the South. They also want a Communist-led government for all of Vietnam. Norton says there is no common ground.

At home, opposition to the war is growing. On October 15, 1969, about 2 million people march in 200 cities. This is called the Moratorium to End the War in Vietnam. The rallies are nonviolent. The marchers want Nixon to bring U.S. forces home sooner.

Nixon and his adviser Henry Kissinger want “peace with honor.” They mean leaving Vietnam in a way that keeps other countries trusting U.S. promises to its allies. What would you choose?

What really happened

Nixon chose the step-by-step plan. He called it “Vietnamization.” That means equipping and training South Vietnamese soldiers and pilots to take over most of the fighting. Norton says Nixon had decided in private that “there’s no way to win the war.” His goal was to leave in a way that kept the trust of U.S. allies.

At the same time, Nixon and Kissinger greatly expanded the bombing of North Vietnam, hoping to pressure its leaders to end the war. In March 1969, Nixon also approved a secret bombing campaign in neighboring Cambodia. Congress did not learn of it until 1970. Norton says Nixon was unmoved by the marches.

U.S. troop numbers fell from 560,000 in 1969 to 50,000 in 1973. In 1973, Nixon ended the draft and created an all-volunteer military. The last U.S. combat troops left Vietnam on March 29, 1973. But the cease-fire soon collapsed. On April 30, 1975, North Vietnamese tanks rolled into Saigon, the capital of South Vietnam. Norton says another 20,000 U.S. troops died between 1969 and the Paris Peace Accords in 1973.

Source: Norton Ch. 28, “Peace with Honor” and “Gradual Withdrawal,” pp. 1296–1297; “Divisions at Home,” p. 1298; “War without End,” p. 1300.

You are there

It is 1971. You are an adviser to President Richard Nixon. Since 1949, Communists led by Mao Zedong have run China. The United States has refused to recognize their government. Instead, it treats Chiang Kai-shek’s government on the island of Taiwan as the real government of China. Nixon himself has supported that policy.

China is the most populous nation in the world, with over 800 million people. Nixon and his adviser Henry Kissinger have a bigger plan for the 1970s. They want friendly relations with the Soviet Union and with China. They want to lower the cost of the arms race and the chance of nuclear war.

Nixon feels the time is right to try something new with China. Both countries are worn out by trouble at home. The United States has anti-war protests. China has the Cultural Revolution. Both also want to stand up to the Soviet Union. But Kissinger is shocked at first. He tells his staff that the president “has taken leave of reality.”

What really happened

Nixon reached out. In 1971, he sent Kissinger on a secret trip to Beijing. Then Nixon announced that he would visit China the next year. He became the first U.S. president to publicly use the name People’s Republic of China. Norton says the Nationalist Chinese in Taiwan felt betrayed, and the Japanese were furious.

In February 1972, Nixon arrived in Beijing in what Norton calls “the week that changed the world.” Americans watched on television as he shook hands and drank toasts with Prime Minister Zhou Enlai and Chairman Mao Zedong. The two nations agreed to scientific and cultural exchanges and outlined steps toward trade.

Norton says Nixon and Kissinger saw opening to China and easing tensions with the Soviet Union as two halves of one plan. Later in 1972, Nixon and Soviet leader Leonid Brezhnev signed the first Strategic Arms Limitation Treaty (SALT I). It limited missiles with nuclear warheads and banned missile-defense systems. It did not end the nuclear arms race.

Dilemma 6 of 6 · 1974

1974: Pardon Nixon or Let the Courts Decide?

You are there

It is 1974. You are an adviser to President Gerald Ford. Ford has been president for less than a month. Richard Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974. He is the only president ever to resign.

Here is what led to it. In June 1972, police caught five burglars in the Democratic Party offices in the Watergate building in Washington, D.C. Nixon and his staff said they had nothing to do with it. Norton says Nixon was lying. Later, people learned that Nixon had secretly recorded talks in the White House. In July 1974, the Supreme Court ruled that Nixon had to hand over all the tapes. A House committee voted to recommend three charges, called articles of impeachment. Then Nixon resigned.

Ford was never elected president or vice president. When he took office, he told the nation, “our long national nightmare is over.” Nixon has not been charged with a crime. Ford can now decide whether to give Nixon a pardon, which would protect him from being charged with any crimes he may have committed in office.

People will argue about this choice. One side says the country cannot move on while a former president is dragged into court again and again. The other side asks why someone should be pardoned before he has been charged. What would you choose?

What really happened

Ford chose to pardon Nixon. Less than a month after taking office, he gave Nixon a “full, free, and absolute pardon” for any crimes he may have committed in office. Ford’s reason was that Americans could not move on while a former president was regularly taken to court and reporters dug into his wrongdoing.

Norton says Ford’s reasoning proved to be a serious miscalculation. The pardon set off a storm of controversy. Protesters outside the White House yelled “Jail Ford!” A House subcommittee asked Ford whether Nixon had made a secret deal for the pardon. Ford strongly denied it. Critics asked why someone who had not been charged with a crime should be pardoned. The New York Times called the pardon “profoundly unwise, divisive, and unjust.” Ford’s approval rating fell from 71 percent to 49 percent in one day, the steepest drop ever recorded.

Norton says the clearest lesson of Watergate was that not even a president is above the law. But many Americans lost faith in elected officials. In 1973, Congress also passed the War Powers Act over Nixon’s veto, to limit the president’s power to send troops into combat.

Source: Norton Ch. 28, “Growing Desperation,” pp. 1309–1310; “Gerald Ford: An Unelected President,” p. 1311.
