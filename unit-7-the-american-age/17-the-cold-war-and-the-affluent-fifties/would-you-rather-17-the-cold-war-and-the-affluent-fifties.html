Dilemma 1 of 6 · 1947

March 1947: Contain the Soviets Patiently or Confront Communism Everywhere?

You are there

It is March 1947. You are an adviser to President Harry Truman. World War II ended less than two years ago. The United States and the Soviet Union fought Hitler as allies. Now they are rivals.

Since 1945, the Soviets have put “puppet” governments in Eastern Europe. These governments take orders from Moscow. The Soviets have banned all parties except the Communists, set up secret police, and taken control of the news and culture. They also broke a promise made at Yalta to allow free elections there.

Many U.S. leaders think the Soviets want to dominate the world. But Norton says the Soviets had real fears too. Germany had invaded Russia twice in the first half of the century, and about 23 million people died.

Now a civil war is raging in Greece. The Greek government, backed by Britain, is fighting rebels led by Communists, who are supported by the Soviets. In February, Britain told the United States it can no longer send money and weapons to Greece. It will pull out its 40,000 troops in five weeks.

Two ideas are on the table. George F. Kennan, an expert on the Soviets, says the United States should “contain” Soviet power. He wants patient, firm efforts over many years, and he wants to help Europe rebuild its economies. He thinks this can work without war. Truman and most of his advisers see containment as a military plan. Truman believes the Soviets understand only military power.

What really happened

Truman chose to confront. In March 1947, he asked Congress for $400 million to help Greece and Turkey. He said the challenge of communism was worldwide, so the United States must support free peoples who were resisting takeover. This became known as the Truman Doctrine. Congress approved the aid in 1947. Kennan thought the promise was “grandiose” because it had no limits. Secretary of State George Marshall thought the speech was too provocative.

Both ideas shaped what came next. In May 1947, Marshall called for a big plan to rebuild Europe, based on Kennan’s suggestions. From 1948 to 1951, the Marshall Plan gave $13 billion to sixteen European nations. The Soviet Union refused to take part. In June 1948, Stalin blocked road, rail, and water traffic into West Berlin. The United States and its allies flew in supplies for 321 days, and no shots were fired. The blockade ended on May 12, 1949. In April 1949, twelve nations signed the North Atlantic Treaty, which created NATO.

In 1950, a secret report called NSC-68 asked for a huge military buildup. In June 1950, North Korea invaded South Korea, and the United States entered the Korean War. Many Americans felt that communism was marching toward world domination. Norton says the war was largely a civil war between two groups fighting for control of a new nation.

Dilemma 2 of 6 · 1949

January 1949: Expand the New Deal or Hold It Back?

You are there

It is January 1949. You are a member of Congress. President Harry Truman has just won a big surprise victory in the 1948 election. Almost everyone had predicted that he would lose.

Truman says the win gives him a mandate, which means voters want him to act. He says every American has a right to expect “a fair deal” from the government. In his State of the Union message, he announces the Fair Deal.

The Fair Deal would protect civil rights for all Americans. It would give more federal aid to schools, add unemployment and retirement benefits, and offer national health insurance. It would help more rural people get electricity and raise the minimum wage. It would also bring 10 million more workers under Social Security and pay for slum clearance and public housing.

Many Republicans and conservative southern Democrats want the government to do less. Some want to end the New Deal programs that Franklin Roosevelt started. They want to cut costs. They say the government should get out of the business of running the economy.

What really happened

Truman got only part of what he asked for. Congress rejected national health insurance, federal aid to education, several civil rights bills, and a new way to support farmers. The same kind of alliance of southern Democrats and Republicans that had worked against Roosevelt in the late 1930s stopped these bills. Congress also refused to repeal the Taft-Hartley Act, which limited unions.

Still, Norton says the Fair Deal was not a complete failure. Truman had expanded Social Security, desegregated the military, and banned racial discrimination in hiring federal employees. The Fair Deal also laid the foundation for programs that reformers pushed about ten years later.

Dwight Eisenhower, a Republican, became president in 1953. He promised to balance the budget and cut government spending, but he did not end the New Deal. He called his plan “dynamic conservatism.” He wanted to be “conservative when it comes to money and liberal when it comes to human beings.” He kept the basic structure of the New Deal. He created the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare, and he extended Social Security to millions of workers who had been left out. He also approved a higher minimum wage and more public housing.

Source: Norton Ch. 25, “Expanding the New Deal,” section starts p. 1124; Ch. 26, “Dynamic Conservatism at Home,” p. 1158.

October 1947: Refuse to Answer Questions or Answer Them?

You are there

It is October 1947. You are a screenwriter in Hollywood. A group in Congress called the House Committee on Un-American Activities, or HUAC, is holding hearings in Los Angeles.

Many Americans fear that Communists directed by the Soviets are working inside the United States. Since 1938, HUAC has claimed that Communist agents are inside the federal government. In March 1947, President Truman ordered background checks on federal workers to make sure they were not Communists.

In May, people charged that the movie business was a “hotbed of communism.” HUAC began a full investigation. It has ordered dozens of well-known actors, producers, and directors to testify.

You have been ordered to testify. You expect to be asked if you are a member of the Communist Party and about your political activities. You think these questions may break your First Amendment rights.

What really happened

Ten witnesses refused to answer questions about their political activities. They said the questions broke their First Amendment rights. They became known as the Hollywood Ten. All ten were cited for contempt of Congress, sent to prison, and blacklisted. Blacklisted means banned from working in the film industry.

Norton says the Red Scare grew. In February 1950, Senator Joseph McCarthy charged that Communists had filled the State Department for years. He claimed to have a list of names, but he never provided it. Norton says McCarthy never uncovered a single Communist agent in the federal government. Even an accusation of Communist ties could ruin a life. Thousands of left-wing Americans were banned from jobs because of past political connections, real or rumored.

Norton also says the fears were not all made up. In 1950, the FBI discovered a spy network that had passed atomic bomb information to the Soviet Union. In 1954, McCarthy charged that the Army itself was “soft” on communism. Some 45 million people watched the televised Army–McCarthy hearings. On December 2, 1954, the Senate voted 67–22 to condemn him, and his influence collapsed.

Source: Norton Ch. 25, “Another Red Scare,” section starts p. 1142; Ch. 26, “The Fall of McCarthy,” p. 1162.

Dilemma 4 of 6 · Late 1950s

The Late 1950s: Follow the Postwar Dream or Turn Away From It?

You are there

It is the late 1950s. You are a young adult in the United States. The country is richer than it has ever been. The government spends huge amounts on defense. Norton says military spending after 1945 was 60 percent of the national budget.

Many people are moving from cities to new suburbs. Of the 13 million homes built from 1948 to 1958, more than 11 million were in the suburbs. About 5 million veterans bought homes with GI Bill loans that needed no down payment. Almost 8 million veterans used the GI Bill for college or job training.

Families are large. The baby boom reached its peak in 1957. American women gave birth to a record 4.3 million babies that year, one every seven seconds.

Some people see problems. In his 1958 book The Affluent Society, economist John Kenneth Galbraith argued that steady economic growth would not solve all of the nation’s big social problems. A small group of writers, poets, painters, and musicians, called the Beats, reject consumer culture and the traditional duties of middle-class life.

What really happened

Most Americans who could follow the postwar path did. Norton says the move from cities to suburbs was the more common migration. Between 1944 and 1956, the GI Bill helped a huge number of poor and working-class Americans reach the middle class. Women’s lives were changing too. In 1950, women were 29 percent of the workforce, and the share rose all decade. About 70 percent of working women had office, assembly-line, or service jobs.

But not everyone had the same choices. Most colleges and universities stayed segregated and refused to admit Black students. Banks used “redlining.” They refused mortgages because of a buyer’s race or ethnicity, so Black veterans were often kept from buying homes in White neighborhoods. The early Levittown house contracts excluded anyone who was not White. Women of color were mostly limited to low-paying jobs such as maids and cooks. Between 1950 and 1960, about 3.6 million White people left the largest cities for suburbs. During the same years, 4.5 million Black people and hundreds of thousands of Puerto Ricans moved into large cities.

Critics said the boom hid problems. Galbraith argued that growth would not solve every social problem. In 1959, one-fourth of Americans had no financial assets. Poverty affected nearly half of the African American population, compared with one-fourth of Whites. The Beats, a small but influential group, rejected the consumer culture and the traditional duties of middle-class life.

Source: Norton Ch. 26, “A People of Plenty,” pp. 1163-1171, and “Cracks in the Picture Window,” pp. 1173-1174.

Dilemma 5 of 6 · 1955

December 1955: Join the Bus Boycott or Rely on the Courts?

You are there

It is December 1955. You are an African American living in Montgomery, Alabama. The city has a law that makes Black bus riders move to the back when White passengers board.

In 1954, the Supreme Court ruled in Brown v. Board of Education. It overturned the “separate but equal” rule for public schools. The Court said that separating students by race made Black children feel inferior. A year later, the Court said integration should move forward “with all deliberate speed.”

But many Southern leaders are fighting the ruling. Senator Harry F. Byrd of Virginia has called for “massive resistance” against federal efforts to enforce integration.

On December 1, a 42-year-old Black seamstress named Rosa Parks boarded the Cleveland Avenue bus. The White driver, James Blake, told her to move to the back. She refused.

Now people in your community must decide how to respond. A young minister, Martin Luther King Jr., teaches nonviolent civil disobedience. That means defying unjust laws and peacefully accepting the consequences. He says there will be no progress without confrontation. The Brown ruling showed that courts could rule against segregation.

What really happened

Many people in Montgomery chose the boycott. It lasted 381 days. African Americans, women and men, organized carpools, used Black-owned taxis, hitchhiked, or walked. Some White people also gave rides.

The boycotters also went to court. On December 20, 1956, they won a federal case they had started against segregation on public buses. The Supreme Court said that “separate but equal” could no longer safely be followed as the law. The next day, King and other African Americans boarded the city buses. Norton says this showed that well-organized, nonviolent Black activism could bring major change.

Change in the courts alone was slow. At the end of 1956, two years after Brown, no Black children attended school with White children in six southern states. By 1960, only 765 of 6,676 school districts in the South had desegregated. In January 1957, King and about sixty Black ministers and leaders met at Ebenezer Church in Atlanta and formed the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC). It promoted nonviolent civil disobedience to desegregate buses across the South. King was its president.

Source: Norton Ch. 26, “The Early Years of the Civil Rights Movement,” pp. 1180-1186.

Dilemma 6 of 6 · 1956

November 1956: Back Your Allies or Demand That They Withdraw?

You are there

It is early November 1956. You are an adviser to President Dwight Eisenhower. Eisenhower and Secretary of State John Foster Dulles say that containing communism is no longer enough. They want to “roll back” communism and help free people who live under Communist rule. They also rely on the threat of nuclear weapons to stop attacks.

On October 23, Hungarians revolted against Communist troops in the streets of Budapest. American radio broadcasts on Radio Free Europe had encouraged them. The Soviet response stunned the world.

At the same time, a crisis is growing in Egypt. Nasser, the Egyptian leader, took control of the Suez Canal Company and refused to let ships headed to Israel use the canal. About two-thirds of Europe’s oil comes through the canal. Britain and France, allies of the United States in NATO, are furious.

Britain and France worked out a plan with Israel to take back the canal. On October 29, Israeli paratroopers dropped into Egypt. Britain and France demanded that the fighting stop. When Egypt rejected this, British warplanes bombed Egyptian airfields. On November 5, British and French soldiers invaded the canal zone.

The president asks you what the United States should do.

What really happened

Eisenhower chose to demand a withdrawal. He shocked Americans and much of the world. He told Britain and France to pull out of the canal zone and Israel to leave the Sinai Peninsula, or face severe economic sanctions. The three nations grudgingly agreed to a cease-fire on November 7. Norton says this showed Eisenhower’s strength, influence, and savvy.

The crisis had lasting effects. Nationalists throughout Africa and Asia saw that Britain and France could not count on American support to hold on to their old colonial empires. In Hungary, many people felt abandoned by the United States. They had believed American broadcasts that said the leaders of the free world would support their rebellion.

Eisenhower faced other tests. In 1954, the French were fighting at Dien Bien Phu in Vietnam. Eisenhower said U.S. military action was “politically impossible” unless Britain joined, and Britain refused. In October 1957, the Soviets launched Sputnik, the first artificial satellite. Congress raised defense spending, put more money into science education, and created NASA in 1958. In a ten-minute speech, Eisenhower warned about the “military-industrial complex.”

Source: Norton Ch. 26, “Reelection and Foreign Crises,” The Suez War, pp. 1195-1197; “Repression in Hungary,” p. 1194; “Foreign Policy in the 1950s,” p. 1187.
