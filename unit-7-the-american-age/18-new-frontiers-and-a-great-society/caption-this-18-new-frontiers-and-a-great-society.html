How this works (15–20 minutes)

  1. Look at each of the 5 images. Read the “What you’re looking at” line.
  2. Write a short, funny caption, like a meme. Pick a caption style if you want one.
  3. Then write 2–3 sentences about what is really going on. Use at least one key term from the lesson. Type the key term in English, exactly as shown.
  4. Save Progress any time. When all 5 are done, press Finished and upload the file to Canvas.

Keep it respectful: make the joke about the setting, the idea, or the situation. Never joke about workers, children, immigrants, enslaved or freed people, Native peoples, anyone’s race or religion, or people who were hurt.

The 1960 TV Debate

Not done

What you’re looking at: A black-and-white photograph from Norton Ch. 27. Two men in dark suits stand at tall wooden podiums on a TV studio stage. Big lights hang above them and more lights sit at the front of the stage. The man on the right points one finger up as he talks. The man on the left looks toward him. This is the 1960 debate between Richard Nixon (left) and John F. Kennedy (right).

Heads up: Real people are in this photo. Aim your joke at the stage, the tall podiums, the lights, or the stools. Never joke about how either man looks, and do not make fun of either side in the election.

  • Look at the stage. Where are the lights? Why would a TV debate need so many?
  • The man on the right is pointing one finger up. What do you think he is saying?

The Man in the Spacesuit

What you’re looking at: A color close-up photograph from Norton Ch. 27. A man sits in a tight space in a shiny silver spacesuit and a helmet. The clear front of the helmet is open, so you can see his face. A thick white hose runs down from the helmet, and straps cross his chest. The name tag on his suit says SHEPARD. This is astronaut Alan Shepard before his 1961 flight.

Heads up: A real person is in this photo. Aim your joke at the shiny suit, the thick hose, the tiny space, or the idea of going to space. Never joke about how he looks or about being afraid.

  • Look at the helmet, the hose, and the straps. What would a person need to stay alive up there?
  • Look at how tight the space is. What does this photo tell you about the risk he took?

Four Students at a Lunch Counter

Not done

What you’re looking at: A black-and-white photograph from Norton Ch. 27. Four young men in coats sit side by side on stools at a lunch counter. One looks straight at the camera. A man in a white uniform and a white paper hat stands behind the counter and looks down. Norton describes four students who sat at an “all-White” lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina, in 1960. This photo shows the Greensboro sit-ins.

Heads up: This is a serious one. These young people took a brave and risky step to challenge segregation. Write one clear line. No jokes about the students, the worker behind the counter, or the cause. Look at the counter, the stools, and how the four sit.

  • Look at the four young men on the stools. What do their faces and the way they sit tell you?
  • Look behind the counter and on the wall. What clues tell you this is a lunch counter?

Two Campaign Buttons

Not done

What you’re looking at: A color photograph from Norton Ch. 27 of two round campaign buttons, one above the other. Both show the face of the same man, Barry Goldwater, the Republican candidate for president in 1964. The top button has a red heart and the words “In Your Heart You Know He’s Right.” The bottom button has a red ring and the words “In Your Guts You Know He’s Nuts.” The bottom one was made by people who were against him. President Lyndon Johnson, a Democrat, was the other candidate.

Heads up: These are real campaign buttons from a hard-fought election. Aim your joke at the slogans, the rhymes, or the idea of wearing your opinion on a button. Never joke about how the man looks, and do not make fun of the people who voted for or against him.

  • Read the two slogans out loud. What is the same about them? What is different?
  • Why would a campaign put a slogan on a button that people wear?
