Dilemma 1 of 5 · October 1962

October 1962: Bomb the Missile Sites or Set Up a Naval Blockade?

You are there

It is October 16, 1962. You are an adviser to President John F. Kennedy. Spy-plane photos show about forty Soviet missile sites and twenty-five jet bombers in Cuba. Cuba is a Communist island nation.

Kennedy and his aides see the missiles and their nuclear warheads as a threat they cannot accept. Somehow, the president must get the Soviets to remove them. The Air Force chief of staff tells him, “You’re in a pretty bad fix, Mr. President.”

Norton says the Soviet leader, Nikita Khrushchev, approved the missiles to protect Cuba from another U.S. attack. The Soviets also felt justified because, after the Bay of Pigs invasion, the United States had put missiles with nuclear warheads in Turkey. Turkey is next to the Soviet Union.

For thirteen days, Kennedy and the National Security Council weigh their options. At first, all of them are military. Now the group has narrowed the choice to two. Kennedy says he wants a response that “lessens the chances of a nuclear exchange.” He also fears that an American attack on Cuba could give the Soviets an excuse to take control of West Berlin.

What really happened

Kennedy chose the blockade. Military advisers had urged bombing the sites, followed by an invasion. But Kennedy remembered the bad advice he got about the Bay of Pigs invasion. The administration called the blockade a “quarantine,” because a blockade is technically an act of war.

On October 22, Kennedy told the nation about the quarantine on television. About 200,000 U.S. soldiers headed to southern Florida. Khrushchev called the blockade “an act of aggression.” On October 24, five Soviet ships, probably carrying more missiles, stopped well short of the quarantine line.

Two days later, Khrushchev offered a deal. The Soviets would remove the missiles in Cuba. In return, the United States made a public promise never to invade Cuba. It also made a secret agreement to remove fifteen U.S. missiles from Turkey. Norton says the world came closer to a nuclear war than ever before or since. Norton also says Soviet military units were prepared to use tactical nuclear weapons if the United States had invaded Cuba. Kennedy did not know that.

After the crisis, both sides took steps to lower tensions. They set up a telephone “hot line” between Washington and Moscow. In September 1963, the Limited Nuclear Test Ban Treaty was ratified. It was the first joint agreement of the Cold War.

Source: Norton Ch. 27, “The Cuban Missile Crisis,” pp. 1213–1214; “Peace for All Time,” p. 1215.

Dilemma 2 of 5 · Mid-1960s

The Mid-1960s: Stay with Nonviolent Protest or Turn to Black Power?

You are there

It is the mid-1960s. You are a young African American activist. You have seen the movement win big victories. Sit-ins began in 1960. In 1963, about 250,000 people marched in Washington, D.C., and Birmingham officials agreed to end their segregationist practices. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 became law.

But life in the big cities has not changed much. About 70 percent of African Americans now live in run-down city areas. Many struggle to find jobs and decent housing. Most face insults from racist White police officers. In August 1965, in the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles, an incident of police brutality set off six nights of rioting. Young people ask how nonviolent protest can change their lives.

Now the movement is divided about what to do next. One path is the one taught by Martin Luther King Jr. and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference. It stresses integration and nonviolent protest. King wrote that “freedom is never voluntarily given by the oppressor; it must be demanded by the oppressed.” But he refused to approve of violence.

The other path is called “Black power.” Its supporters talk about racial pride, self-respect, and self-defense. They say that nonviolence has not worked for Black people in the cities. Malcolm X, its best-known voice, said African Americans had a right to meet police brutality with force.

What really happened

The movement did not pick just one path. Both continued, and they pulled apart. Norton says Black Power “emerged as the alternative” to the integrationist, nonviolent ideas of King and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference. Malcolm X was killed on February 21, 1965. By 1966, “Black power” was a rallying cry for many young activists. Stokely Carmichael became head of SNCC, removed White members, and turned away from nonviolence.

In 1966, Huey Newton and Bobby Seale founded the Black Panther Party in Oakland, California. The party never had more than 2,000 members. It said Black people had a right to defend themselves from police harassment. It also ran Free Breakfast for Children programs, job-training programs, and free health clinics. More than forty chapters opened in cities. Norton says the Panthers also advocated revolutionary violence. From 1967 to 1969, shootouts killed nine police officers and ten Panthers. The FBI worked to undermine the party, and it fell apart by 1982.

The nonviolent path had won real change. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 became law. By the 1970s, Deep South states that had fought civil rights changes the hardest led the nation in the number of Black elected officials.

Source: Norton Ch. 27, “Black Power,” pp. 1230–1232; “Birmingham,” pp. 1223–1224; Civil Rights Act (p. 1235); Voting Rights Act (p. 1241).

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1965

1965: Limit Health Insurance to Seniors or Also Help the Poor?

You are there

It is 1965. You are an adviser to President Lyndon Johnson. Johnson has a big goal. In May 1964, he described a “Great Society” that would end poverty and racial injustice and provide “abundance and liberty for all.”

Health care is one of the big fights. President Kennedy wanted medical insurance for older Americans. But conservative southern Democrats joined Republicans in Congress and blocked it. Now Johnson and the Democrats have the votes to pass a health bill.

Not everyone agrees on how big the bill should be. The American Medical Association (AMA), a group of doctors, has joined Republicans to limit the bill to people over age sixty-five. Others want to help poor Americans of all ages with their medical costs, too.

What really happened

Johnson and the Democrats did both. The bill that passed was limited to people over sixty-five. It came to be called Medicare. Johnson also created a program of federal grants to the states to help pay medical costs for the poor of all ages. It is known as Medicaid. Both were passed in 1965.

Medicare and Medicaid were part of many Great Society laws. Others included the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 (the War on Poverty), the Immigration and Nationality Services Act of 1965, and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Norton says the Great Society and War on Poverty did not end urban or rural poverty. This was partly because the Vietnam War soon took priority and drained away funding, and partly because Johnson’s speeches promised more than government could do. But there were lasting successes. Infant deaths have dropped, college completion has risen, malnutrition is lower, and far fewer older Americans live in poverty and without health care.

Source: Norton Ch. 27, “The Great Society” and “Health Insurance, Housing, and Higher Education,” p. 1238; “Assessing the Great Society,” p. 1242; “A Contrary Congress,” p. 1209.

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1965

1965: Send Many More Troops to Vietnam or Keep the U.S. Role Limited?

You are there

It is 1965. You are an adviser to President Lyndon Johnson. The United States has backed South Vietnam against Communist North Vietnam for years. President Kennedy sent weapons, money, and about 16,000 military “advisers.” Kennedy said, “If I tried to pull out, we would have another Joe McCarthy red scare on our hands.”

South Vietnam’s government has been unstable. In November 1963, Vietnamese generals took over with U.S. approval and murdered the country’s leader, Ngo Dinh Diem. Norton says his successors were worse and began fighting each other. Meanwhile, Viet Cong guerrilla fighters, backed by North Vietnam, are winning the fight against South Vietnamese forces. In August 1964, Congress passed the Tonkin Gulf Resolution, which lets the president “take all necessary measures” to protect U.S. forces.

Many leaders believe the United States must contain communism everywhere. Johnson thinks that losing South Vietnam would “shatter my presidency.” But he also has doubts. Before his death, Kennedy said of Vietnam, “it’s their war. . . . We can help them as advisers but they have to win it.”

What really happened

Johnson chose to send more troops. Norton says his fear of looking weak against communism outweighed his doubts. By the end of 1965, there were 184,000 U.S. troops in Vietnam. In 1966 there were 385,000. In 1969, at the height of the war, there were 542,000.

Johnson explained his reason in a 1965 speech: “We are there because we have a promise to keep.” Norton says his decision to “Americanize” the war grew from mistaken Cold War beliefs, including the commitment to contain communism everywhere.

Norton also says the story Johnson told in August 1964 about attacks on two U.S. warships in the Gulf of Tonkin was later shown to be false and misleading, because the U.S. ships had fired first. The Tonkin Gulf Resolution was Johnson’s official reason for escalation. Norton calls it a “blank check” to wage war without a declaration of war.

The war grew without a victory in sight. After the Tet offensive in 1968, Johnson announced he would not run for another term. Norton also says the Vietnam War drained funding from the War on Poverty.

Source: Norton Ch. 27, “Kennedy and Vietnam,” pp. 1215–1216; “The Tragedy of Vietnam,” pp. 1243–1245.

Dilemma 5 of 5 · Fall 1968

Fall 1968: Vote for Humphrey or Vote for Nixon?

You are there

It is October 1968. You are a voter in the United States. The year has been the most traumatic of the decade. In January, the Tet offensive made many people doubt that the Vietnam War was nearly won. On March 31, President Johnson announced he would not run again. Martin Luther King Jr. was murdered. Riots broke out in more than 100 cities, and forty-six people were killed, all but five of them African Americans.

In August, at the Democratic National Convention in Chicago, almost 20,000 police officers and National Guardsmen faced thousands of anti-war protesters outside the hall.

Two candidates stand out. Hubert H. Humphrey is Johnson’s “faithful vice president” and the Democratic nominee. His party passed the Civil Rights Act, the Voting Rights Act, Medicare, and Medicaid. Richard Nixon, the Republican, says he speaks for the “silent majority” of working- and middle-class Americans. He promises “peace with honor” in Vietnam and to “bring us together.”

What really happened

On November 5, 1968, Richard Nixon and his running mate, Governor Spiro Agnew of Maryland, won by about 500,000 votes. That was a margin of about 1 percentage point. The electoral vote was 301 for Nixon and 191 for Humphrey. A third candidate, George Wallace, won 46 electoral votes, all from the Deep South.

Norton says Democrats kept control of both the House and the Senate. But the election showed a warning sign for the party. White wage workers, the backbone of the Democratic Party, were shifting to the Republicans.

Nixon had appealed to the “silent majority.” Norton says they were working- and middle-class Americans who viewed the street protesters with contempt. At the end of 1968, a divided country looked to Nixon to keep his promises of “peace with honor” in Vietnam and to “bring us together.”

Source: Norton Ch. 27, “1968: A Traumatic Year,” p. 1251; “Chicago and Miami,” pp. 1252–1253; “Richard Nixon Triumphant,” pp. 1253–1254.
