Dilemma 1 of 6 · 1979

1979: Send in the Military or Try Talks and Pressure First?

You are there

It is November 1979. You are an adviser to President Jimmy Carter. In January, revolutionaries in Iran forced out the shah, the ruler who was friendly to the United States. In October, Carter let the shah come to the United States for medical treatment. This made many Iranian revolutionaries furious.

On November 4, an Iranian mob stormed the U.S. embassy in Tehran. They took sixty-six diplomats, Marines, and staff, including fifty-two American citizens. Iran’s leader, Ayatollah Khomeini, says he will free them only if the shah and his money are sent back to Iran.

Americans watch the news about the hostages every day. Many are angry, and many members of Congress demand a military response. Others fear that any military action would get the hostages killed.

What really happened

Carter chose to hold back at first. Norton says he feared that military action would get the hostages killed. He asked the United Nations to help, but Khomeini scoffed at the UN’s efforts. Carter then froze Iranian money in the United States and asked Europe to join a trade ban on Iran. Allies did not want to lose Iranian oil, so the trade limits worked only partly.

As the crisis dragged on, Carter approved a risky rescue attempt by U.S. commandos on April 24, 1980. It was called off when several helicopters broke down. Eight U.S. soldiers died when a helicopter hit a transport plane in the Iranian desert.

Iran freed the hostages on Inauguration Day 1981, only hours after Ronald Reagan took office. The crisis lasted 444 days. Norton says it all but ended Carter’s chances for reelection.

Source: Norton Ch. 29, “Crisis in Iran,” pp. 1325–1327.

Dilemma 2 of 6 · 1981

1981: Stay the Course or Raise Some Taxes?

You are there

It is the fall of 1981. You are an adviser to President Ronald Reagan. In August, he signed a big tax cut called the Economic Recovery Tax Act. It cut personal income taxes by 25 percent. It also lowered the top tax rate from 70 percent to 50 percent.

Reagan believed that lower tax rates would help the economy grow. People and businesses would spend and invest more. His plan said this would bring in enough new tax money to pay for the cuts. The idea is called supply-side economics. Reporters called it Reaganomics.

It is not working yet. The federal budget deficit is growing. A deficit means the government spends more than it takes in. By November, the economy is officially in a recession. The military budget is also rising fast. Budget director David Stockman warned that the budget was heading for “a crash landing.”

Some advisers say: keep the tax cuts, cut spending, and give the plan time. Others say the government needs “revenue enhancements.” That is another way of saying higher taxes. What would you choose?

What really happened

Reagan’s advisers, including Stockman, finally convinced him that the government needed “revenue enhancements.” With his support, Congress passed a tax bill in 1982 that would raise almost $100 billion. Norton calls this a sign of Reagan’s pragmatism. He also stayed with his plan, and the cuts to domestic spending fell far short of what he had promised.

The slump did not end right away. In the 1982 elections, Democrats won twenty-six more seats in the House. By the summer of 1983, a strong recovery was under way. Norton says this was partly because of more government spending and lower interest rates, and partly because of lower tax rates. Inflation and unemployment went down.

But the deficit kept growing. Norton says the federal deficit almost tripled during Reagan’s two terms.

Source: Norton Ch. 29, “Reaganomics” and “Managing the Budget,” pp. 1332–1333; “Reagan’s Half-Hearted Revolution,” p. 1335.

Dilemma 3 of 6 · 1986

1986: Give Up SDI or Keep It?

You are there

It is October 1986. You are an adviser to President Ronald Reagan at a meeting in Reykjavik, Iceland. The United States and the Soviet Union have thousands of nuclear weapons. Reagan wants to end the Cold War. His plan is “peace through strength.” He built up the military so the Soviets would see they could not win a nuclear war.

In 1983, Reagan started the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI). It is a plan for a defense that uses satellites and lasers to stop Soviet missiles in flight. Critics call it “Star Wars.” Many scientists say it can never be built. Even Reagan’s own secretary of state, George Shultz, called it “lunacy.” Reagan’s side says SDI puts pressure on the Soviets. Congress has paid for the first stage, and the Soviets have started an expensive research effort of their own.

The Soviet leader is Mikhail Gorbachev. He took power in 1985. His country has a weak economy and is losing a war in Afghanistan. In ten hours of talks, Reagan says, “It would be fine with me if we eliminated all nuclear weapons.” Gorbachev answers, “We can do that.” Then Gorbachev demands that the United States give up SDI.

What really happened

Reagan kept SDI. Norton says he walked out of the meeting when Gorbachev demanded that the United States give up SDI. By the end of the meeting, the two sides were still far apart.

But the talks did not end for good. In 1987, Gorbachev said he was willing to consider cuts in nuclear weapons on both sides. After nine months of hard talks, Reagan and Gorbachev signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty in Washington, D.C., on December 9, 1987. It eliminated thousands of intermediate-range missiles.

Norton calls it the most sweeping reduction in nuclear weapons in history. Norton says it came from Reagan’s show of strength together with a new kind of Soviet leader in Gorbachev.

Source: Norton Ch. 29, “A Massive Defense Buildup,” p. 1336; “The Reykjavik Summit,” p. 1339; “The INF Treaty,” p. 1340.

Dilemma 4 of 6 · 1990

1990: Defend Kuwait or Stay Out?

You are there

It is August 1990. You are an adviser to President George H. W. Bush. The Cold War is ending, and now a new crisis has begun. Iraq’s dictator, Saddam Hussein, has invaded Kuwait. Kuwait is a tiny neighbor with a lot of oil.

Hussein was upset that Kuwait had made more oil than its agreements with other oil-producing nations allowed. That made oil prices drop. Iraq was deep in debt and needed its oil money.

Most foreign leaders assume that nothing can or should be done to protect Kuwait. The United States has to decide what to do.

What really happened

Bush chose to act. He called the invasion “naked aggression” and said, “this will not stand.” He sent warplanes and troops to defend Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, and he asked other nations to join. British forces joined, and so did Arab troops from several nations.

Iraq refused to leave. On January 12, 1991, Congress authorized the use of force to free Kuwait. The United Nations Security Council, including Russia and China, agreed that Iraq must withdraw.

Four days later, U.S. forces and forces from thirty-three other nations began Operation Desert Storm. During the next six weeks, Iraqi soldiers surrendered by the thousands. On February 28, Bush called for a cease-fire, and Iraq accepted. Hussein stayed in power. Norton says Bush believed that removing Hussein would only have brought more instability to the region.

Source: Norton Ch. 29, “The Gulf War,” p. 1353.

Dilemma 5 of 6 · 1993–1994

Clinton’s First Term: Approve NAFTA or Vote No?

You are there

It is early in Bill Clinton’s presidency. You are a member of Congress. You must vote on the North American Free Trade Agreement, or NAFTA.

NAFTA is a trade deal with Canada and Mexico. The Bush administration negotiated it. Norton says it was largely Ronald Reagan’s idea. If it passes, North America will be the largest free-trade zone in the world. In a free-trade zone, goods move across borders more easily. President Clinton urges Congress to approve it.

Opponents want tariffs instead. A tariff is a tax on goods from other countries. Tariffs would discourage people from buying cheaper foreign products, especially from Mexico. A large group of Democrats, mostly labor union members and Southerners, are against NAFTA. They fear that textile mills, which make cloth, would lose business and millions of jobs to “cheap labor” countries.

What really happened

Congress approved NAFTA. Norton says Clinton won with solid Republican support. A sizable minority of Democrats, mostly labor union members and Southerners, opposed it.

Norton says that the opponents’ fear about textile mills came true. Textile mills did lose business to “cheap labor” countries.

Norton also says that globalization became a feature of the new economy. Globalization is the process by which people, goods, information, and cultural tastes move across national borders.

Dilemma 6 of 6 · 2001

2001: Vote for the Patriot Act or Vote No?

You are there

It is the fall of 2001. You are a member of Congress. On September 11, terrorists hijacked four airliners. About 3,000 people died, including more than 400 firefighters, police officers, and emergency workers. Norton says many Americans felt grief, anger, and fear. Officials worry that terrorists might attack with biological, chemical, or even nuclear weapons.

President George W. Bush asks Congress to approve the USA Patriot Act. Norton says it gave government agencies new powers. One was the power to listen in on private talks between prison inmates and their lawyers. Norton also describes new rules that let suspected terrorists be tried in secret military courts. They could be held at a military prison at Guantánamo in Cuba, with no end date and no access to lawyers.

Supporters point to the danger of another attack. Civil liberties groups say these measures put constitutional rights and protections in danger.

What really happened

Congress approved the USA Patriot Act in 2001. Norton says Bush convinced Congress to pass it. Norton also says that civil liberties groups voiced concerns that the measures put constitutional rights and protections in danger. But most Americans supported the measures.

Bush also created the Department of Homeland Security. Another new agency, the Transportation Security Administration (TSA), took over the job of screening airline passengers for weapons and bombs.
