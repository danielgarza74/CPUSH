Dilemma 1 of 5 · 1700s

The 1700s: Sign an Indenture to Go to America?

You are there

It is the early 1700s. You are a poor worker in England, Ireland, or Scotland. Land in Europe is crowded and expensive. You cannot save enough money to buy a farm, and you cannot pay for a ticket to America.

A ship’s captain offers you a deal called an indenture. It is a contract. He pays for your trip. In return, you promise to work for a fixed number of years, usually three to seven. In America, land is cheap and workers are scarce.

But you have heard that life for servants is hard, and that servants have few rights. Many do not live to finish their contracts. What would you choose?

What really happened

Many people signed. Of about 500,000 English immigrants who came to America between 1610 and 1775, about 350,000 came as indentured servants.

Norton says life was harsh. Servants got food and a bed, but they had few rights, and many died before their contracts ended. Still, the contract ended. When it did, a servant could claim “freedom dues,” such as a little money, tools, clothing, and food, and sometimes a small piece of land. Some former servants did very well. By 1637, fifteen former servants sat in the Maryland Assembly.

Not everyone chose. Norton also says that some servants did not come willingly. Some homeless children in London were kidnapped and sold into servitude. Parliament also sent about 50,000 convicted criminals to the colonies.

Source: Norton Ch. 3, “Society and Economy in the Colonies” (Indentured Servitude) and “The Demography of the Early Colonies” (Population Growth).

Dilemma 2 of 5 · 1740

1740: Go to the Revival, or Stay With Your Minister?

You are there

It is 1740. You live in a colonial town. Your local church has a minister, and most families go every week. Where you live, the government supports one official church with taxes.

A traveling preacher has come to the area. These preachers are called itinerants. He says that many local ministers are not good enough. He says that people must be “reborn” in their beliefs and in how they live. Big crowds are gathering in barns and open fields.

At the same time, many educated people are talking about a new way of thinking called the Enlightenment. It celebrates reason and science. Your neighbors are arguing, and the argument is dividing families. Where would you rather be on Sunday?

What really happened

The revival, called the Great Awakening, spread up and down the Atlantic coast. Jonathan Edwards said people needed to have their “hearts touched,” not just their heads filled with new knowledge. George Whitefield visited the colonies seven times between 1738 and 1748 and preached to huge crowds.

Norton says the highly emotional gatherings divided congregations, towns, and families. They also helped build new groups, especially Baptists and Methodists. Even Benjamin Franklin, who was skeptical, admitted that people were willing to attend sermons as never before.

Both movements changed American thinking. Enlightenment ideas about reason, natural rights, and consent of the governed later helped colonial leaders justify a revolution.

Source: Norton Ch. 3, “First Stirrings of a Common Colonial Culture” (The Enlightenment; The Great Awakening).

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1754

1754: Unite the Colonies, or Keep Them Separate?

You are there

It is the summer of 1754. You are a delegate at a meeting in Albany, New York. The French and the British are fighting for the lands of the Ohio valley, and fighting has already started on the frontier. Whoever controls that land could control the whole continent.

Benjamin Franklin has a plan. The colonies would band together. The king would appoint a president. Each colony’s assembly would send two to seven delegates to a “grand council” that could make laws. The union would handle dealings with Indigenous nations.

Other delegates and many colonial legislatures like their own powers just as they are. They want only a military alliance against attacks. What would you support?

What really happened

The Albany Congress approved the Albany Plan of Union in 1754. But it did not become law. Norton says the plan was “too radical.” British officials and the colonial legislatures wanted to keep their own powers, so they rejected it.

Franklin also drew a famous cartoon in 1754 urging the colonies to unite. It was printed on May 9, 1754, in the Pennsylvania Gazette.

War came anyway. In 1755 Britain sent 1,000 soldiers to the colonies. Norton says the colonists favored using force against the French, but later opposed using British soldiers to enforce rules on the colonies. Twenty years later, in 1774, representatives from twelve colonies met in the First Continental Congress. Norton says that never before had so many colonies met to coordinate joint resistance.

Source: Norton Ch. 4, “Warfare in the Colonies” (The Albany Plan; War in North America) and “The Road to the American Revolution” (The First Continental Congress).

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1765

1765: Pay the Stamp Tax, or Protest and Boycott?

You are there

It is 1765. You are a printer in a colonial port city. Britain won the French and Indian War, but the war left it with huge debts. It is also keeping troops in America. Prime Minister George Grenville says Americans should help pay for the soldiers who defend them.

Parliament has passed the Stamp Act. You must buy special paper from London with a government stamp on it. You must use that paper for newspapers, pamphlets, legal papers, licenses, insurance policies, college diplomas, even playing cards.

British officials say Parliament’s power over the colonies is complete. Many colonists say they have no elected representatives in Parliament, so Parliament cannot tax them. What would you do?

What really happened

Many colonists chose protest. Norton says the Stamp Act aroused intense resentment and resistance, and that critics repeated the slogan “No taxation without representation.” Groups called Sons of Liberty formed in every colony. Thousands signed agreements not to buy British goods. Women called the Daughters of Liberty stopped buying British cloth and tea.

The boycott worked. Imports of British goods fell by 40 percent, and thousands of English workers lost their jobs. In February 1766 Parliament repealed the Stamp Act.

But Parliament also passed the Declaratory Act, which claimed the power to govern the colonies “in all cases whatsoever.” In 1767 it passed the Townshend Acts, with new taxes on glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea.

Source: Norton Ch. 4, “Tightening of Control over the British Colonies” (The Stamp Act; The Whig Point of View; Protests in the Colonies; Colonial Unity; Repeal of the Stamp Act).

Dilemma 5 of 5 · 1776

Early 1776: Seek Peace With the King, or Declare Independence?

You are there

It is early 1776. You are a delegate in Philadelphia. Fighting started at Lexington and Concord last April. Until then, both sides thought the other would back down. Now it is a war.

Last summer, Congress sent the king the Olive Branch Petition. It asked him to negotiate. He refused to read it. He called the Americans “traitorous” and said they were “open and avowed” enemies.

A new pamphlet called Common Sense says it is time to break away. Many colonists still hope to stay under the king. And the king now calls the Americans traitors. What would you choose?

What really happened

Congress chose independence. In January 1776, Thomas Paine published Common Sense. It attacked the king directly, and more than 150,000 copies were circulating within three months. Norton says it convinced many anxious Americans that independence was necessary.

On July 4, 1776, fifty-six members of Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence. Thomas Jefferson, age thirty-three, wrote the main draft. It said that governments get their power from “the consent of the governed.” The delegates knew they were likely to be hanged if captured.

Norton also points out a contradiction. The Declaration said that “all men are created equal,” but that idea did not apply to the many enslaved people in the colonies.

Source: Norton Ch. 4, “The Road to the American Revolution” (Open and Avowed Enemies; Independence; Breaking the Bonds of Empire; Jefferson’s Declaration).
