Dilemma 1 of 5 · 1619

1619: Sail to Virginia, or Stay in England?

You are there

It is 1619. You are a farmer in England. In your area, landlords have found it more profitable to raise sheep, so many farmworkers have lost their land. Work is hard to find, and you have no land of your own.

Last year the Virginia Company started a new offer called a headright. If you buy a share in the company and pay for your trip, you get fifty acres of land when you arrive. You get fifty more acres for each servant you bring. This year the company also promises that settlers will have the “rights of Englishmen,” including a legislature elected by the people.

But you have heard that the trip is dangerous and that life in Jamestown is hard. Many colonists have gotten sick or starved. What would you choose?

What really happened

Many people went. Norton says that by 1624, 8,000 English men, women, and children had migrated to Jamestown. Only 1,132 were still alive, and many of them were in “a sickly and desperate state.”

Tobacco became the most profitable crop in Virginia and Maryland. Settlers began growing it to sell in England in 1612. By 1620 the colony shipped 50,000 pounds a year. By 1670 Virginia and Maryland sent 15 million pounds a year. But tobacco farming needed more land and more workers.

The company itself did not last. In 1624 the Virginia Company declared bankruptcy, and Virginia became a royal colony with governors chosen by the king.

The land was not empty. About 14,000 Powhatan people lived in eastern Virginia. As settlers wanted more land for tobacco, conflict grew. In 1622 Indigenous people attacked settlements along the James River to push the English back.

Source: Norton Ch. 2, “The English Background” (People and Profits) and “Settling the American Colonies” (Jamestown; The Powhatan Confederacy; Tobacco; Governance).

Dilemma 2 of 5 · 1634

1634: A Refuge for Catholics, or a Colony Open to All?

You are there

It is 1634. You are Cecilius Calvert, the second Lord Baltimore. Your father, George Calvert, asked the king for a charter for a colony north of Virginia. He died before the king acted, so the charter went to you. The colony is named Maryland. It is a proprietary colony, which means one person controls it.

You are a Roman Catholic. You see Maryland as a refuge for English Catholics, who want a place to live without being persecuted.

But you also want the colony to make money, and you do not want to anger the Protestants. Your brother Leonard will be the first governor. What kind of colony should Maryland be?

What really happened

Calvert tried to do both. Norton says he told his brother Leonard to make sure that Catholic colonists worshipped in private and stayed “silent” about religious matters.

But the Calverts could not attract enough Roman Catholics to build a colony that could support itself. So they changed their plan. They offered Protestants “a quiet life sweetened with ease and plenty” on small farms, free from religious persecution. The Calverts ruled with the consent of the freemen, the men who owned property.

In the end, Maryland succeeded more quickly than Virginia because it focused on growing tobacco from the start.

Norton lists a welcome to many peoples as one advantage of the English colonies. Leaders of the Dutch and non-Puritan English colonies welcomed people of many nationalities and religions. By the early 1700s, English America had become the most populous, prosperous, and powerful of the European empires’ colonies.

Source: Norton Ch. 2, “Settling the American Colonies” (Maryland) and “Thriving Colonies”.

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1636

1636: Stay in Massachusetts Bay, or Follow Roger Williams?

You are there

It is 1636. You live in the Massachusetts Bay Colony, near Boston. You came with the Puritans, who left England to build a model Christian society. Their leader, Governor John Winthrop, called it “a City upon a Hill.” Only people who can show that God has given them grace may be full church members. Leaders believe that the government should enforce religious beliefs and keep order.

Winthrop says that a democracy is “the worst of all forms of government.” Another leader, the Reverend John Cotton, asks, “If the people be governors, who shall be governed?”

Then a man named Roger Williams speaks up. He says that people should be free to worship God in their own way. He says that church and government must be completely separate. “Forced worship,” he says, “stinks in God’s nostrils.” Leaders call his ideas “dangerous opinions.” Where do you stand?

What really happened

Massachusetts Bay’s leaders chose to enforce their beliefs. Norton says that Governor Winthrop and the General Court banished Williams to England. Before he could be sent away, he and his wife slipped off during a blizzard and found shelter with the Narragansett people.

Williams studied the Narragansett language and defended their rights as human beings. In 1636 he bought land from them and started the town of Providence. Rhode Island became the most democratic colony. It was governed by heads of households, not by church members. New people could become full citizens by a majority vote, and it welcomed people who had fled religious persecution. Many Massachusetts Puritans saw it as a refuge for rogues. A Dutch visitor called it “the sewer of New England.”

Both kinds of colony lasted. Norton says that the Puritans who had fled persecution in England did not hesitate to persecute people of other faiths in New England. By 1700, New England’s White population was larger than Maryland’s and Virginia’s combined.

Source: Norton Ch. 2, “Settling the American Colonies” (New England; Massachusetts Bay; Rhode Island).

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1664

1664: Defend New Amsterdam, or Accept the English Offer?

You are there

It is 1664. You live in New Amsterdam, a Dutch trading town on Manhattan Island. The Dutch built it for the fur trade and for profit. One resident says, “Everyone here is a trader.” People of many countries and religions live here, and barely half of the colony’s residents are Dutch.

King Charles II of England has decided to take New Netherland. An English fleet is coming. Governor Stuyvesant calls on the residents to defend the colony.

Many residents do not want to fight. The English surround the town and threaten the “absolute ruin and destruction” of about 1,500 people. What would you choose?

What really happened

The governor gave up. In 1664, Stuyvesant surrendered the colony without firing a shot.

The Dutch negotiated an unusual agreement. New Netherlanders kept their property, churches, language, and local officials. The English renamed the town New York, in honor of James Stuart, the Duke of York, the king’s brother, who led the invasion. The king then gave his brother the whole Dutch region.

The Dutch colony was small. Norton says that in 1660 there were 5,000 colonists in New Netherland, compared with 58,000 in New England, Virginia, and Maryland. The English colonies had far more people than the Spanish, the French, or the Dutch colonies.

Source: Norton Ch. 2, “Settling the American Colonies” (The Dutch Republic and Trade) and “The English Background” (People and Profits).

Dilemma 5 of 5 · 1690s

The 1690s: Keep Fighting, or Make Peace?

You are there

It is the 1690s. You are a leader of the Iroquois League, known to its members as the Haudenosaunee, or Great Peace. About fifty sachems (chiefs) govern about 12,000 people. Your capital is Onondaga. The League is strong, and Dutch and English traders must work with you to get beaver pelts.

For more than twenty years, war has raged across the Great Lakes region. On one side are the Iroquois, supported by English and Dutch fur traders. On the other side are the Algonquian and Huron peoples and their French allies. Your people control a huge area, from the St. Lawrence River to Tennessee.

Now the French and their Indigenous allies have struck back. They have destroyed your crops and villages. They have spread smallpox and other diseases. The number of men in the League has dropped by more than one-third. What would you choose?

What really happened

The Iroquois League chose peace. Norton says that, facing extermination, the Iroquois made peace with the French in 1701.

During the first half of the 1700s, they stayed out of the almost constant wars between the English and the French. This let them play the two European powers against each other. They also built a thriving fur trade for themselves.

Norton says that the Iroquois story is just one of the ways Indigenous peoples and colonists dealt with each other’s presence. Some of those ways were peaceful, and some were violent.

Source: Norton Ch. 2, “Indigenous Peoples and English Settlers” (The Iroquois League).
