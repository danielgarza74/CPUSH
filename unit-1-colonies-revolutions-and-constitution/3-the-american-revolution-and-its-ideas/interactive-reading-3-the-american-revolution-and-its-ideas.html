A Revolution and a Civil War

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 1: Describe the ways in which the American Revolution was also a civil war.

IN MY WORDS: How could a war against Britain also be a war of Americans against Americans?

Reading ALoyalists and Patriots

The Revolution was as much a deeply divisive civil war among bitterly opposed American factions (including the Indigenous peoples allied with both sides) as it was a struggle against Great Britain. It sundered families and friends, towns and cities. . . . The colonists were divided into three groups: (1) Patriots, who formed the Continental Army and fought in state militias; (2) Loyalists, or Tories, siding with Britain; and (3) a middle group that sought to remain neutral but were eventually swayed by the Patriots.

The Loyalists viewed the Revolution as an act of treason. They were most numerous in the seaport cities and the Carolinas, and they came from all walks of life. . . . The Patriots supported the war because they realized that the only way to protect their liberty was to separate themselves from British control. They also wanted to establish an American republic that would convert them from being subjects of a king to being citizens with the power to elect their own government and pursue their own interests.

Word help: militias = local groups of citizen-soldiers · neutral = not taking a side · divisive = splitting people apart · factions = groups that disagree · sundered = tore apart · treason = betraying your country · republic = a government run by elected leaders

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “American Society at War” (Loyalists; Patriots).

Fighting the War

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 2: Explain the challenges faced by both British and American military leaders in fighting the Revolutionary War.

IN MY WORDS: Name one problem the British army had and one problem the American army had.

Reading BBritish Military Power

The British Empire sent 35,000 soldiers and half of its huge navy to suppress the American rebellion. The British also hired foreign soldiers (mercenaries), as evidenced by the 30,000 German soldiers who served in the British armies. Most were from the German state of Hesse-Cassel; thus, Americans called them Hessians. . . . The war’s increasing costs—in human lives and war debt—demoralized the British. Another problem facing the British was that its government in the colonies under Lord Frederick North lacked a consistent war strategy.

Word help: suppress = stop by force · mercenaries = soldiers paid to fight for another country · demoralized = took away hope · consistent = steady, the same

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “Mobilizing for War” (British Military Power).

Reading CThe Continental Army

Before the war, citizen-soldiers (militiamen) were civilians summoned as needed from their farms and shops. During the Revolution, however, militiamen were at times unreliable and ungovernable. . . . Washington knew that militiamen alone could not win the war. He therefore convinced the Continental Congress to create a Continental Army with full-time, well-trained soldiers. . . . Some 5,000 people of African descent, virtually all formerly enslaved, also served.

Financing the Revolution was much harder for the Americans than it was for Great Britain. Lacking the power to impose taxes, Congress could only ask the states to provide funds for the national government. . . . Both the British and the Americans recruited Indigenous people to fight. But the British were far more successful at it, largely because they had long-standing relationships with chieftains and promised to protect ancestral Indigenous lands.

Word help: civilians = people who are not soldiers · summoned = called · unreliable = could not be trusted · financing = paying for · impose = force on people

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “Mobilizing for War” (The Continental Army; Problems of Finance and Supply; Indigenous Americans and the Revolution).

Source 1“The Times That Try Men’s Souls” (Primary Source)

By December 1776, Washington had only 3,000 soldiers left. Thomas Paine wrote The American Crisis to keep Americans fighting.

“These are the times that try men’s souls: The summer soldier and the sunshine patriot will, in this crisis, shrink from the service of his country; but he that stands it NOW deserves the love and thanks of man and woman. Tyranny, like Hell, is not easily conquered. Yet we have this consolation with us, that the harder the conflict, the more glorious the triumph.”

Word help: try = test · summer soldier = someone who fights only when it is easy · shrink from = back away from · tyranny = cruel rule · consolation = comfort · triumph = victory

Thomas Paine, The American Crisis, December 1776. In Norton, Ch. 5, “Mobilizing for War” (Thomas Paine: “The Times That Try Men’s Souls”).

Turning Points

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 3: Identify key turning points in the Revolutionary War, and explain how they changed the direction of the war.

IN MY WORDS: What is a turning point in a war?

Reading DTrenton (1776)

On a “fearfully cold and raw” Christmas night in 1776, Washington secretly led some 2,400 men—including ten men of African descent—packed into forty-foot-long boats and maintaining a “profound silence,” across the ice-clogged Delaware River into New Jersey. Near sunrise at Trenton, the Americans surprised 1,500 sleeping Hessians. The Battle of Trenton . . . was a total rout. . . . The victories at Princeton and Trenton saved the cause of independence and shifted the war’s momentum.

Word help: profound = deep · rout = a total defeat · momentum = the force that keeps something moving forward

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “Mobilizing for War” (A Desperate Gamble).

Reading ESaratoga and the French Alliance (1777–1778)

On October 17, 1777, Burgoyne surrendered, turning over 5,800 troops, 7,000 muskets, and 42 cannons. . . . The surprising victory at Saratoga was a strategic turning point for the new nation because it enabled a Treaty of Alliance with France, which was hungry for revenge after losing four wars to the British in the previous eighty years.

Word help: Burgoyne = the British general who led an army from Canada · surrendered = gave up · strategic = important for winning · alliance = partnership

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “Setbacks for the British (1777–1781)” (The Campaign of 1777; The Crucial Alliance with France).

Reading FValley Forge (1777–1778)

For the American army at Valley Forge, near Philadelphia, the winter of 1777–1778 was a time of intense suffering. . . . They were hungry, and their 900 log-and-mud huts offered little protection from the icy winds. . . . More than 2,500 soldiers died at Valley Forge, another 1,000 deserted . . . For that he turned to an energetic Prussian soldier of fortune, Friedrich Wilhelm, Baron von Steuben, who taught the Americans how to march, shoot, and attack in formation, and educated them on basic hygiene.

Word help: intense = very strong · deserted = ran away from the army · Prussian = from Prussia, a German state · formation = an organized group shape · hygiene = keeping clean to stay healthy

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “Setbacks for the British (1777–1781)” (Valley Forge and Stalemate).

Reading GSpain Helps, and Yorktown (1781)

In 1779, Spain, like France, had forged an alliance with the United States. . . . Bernardo de Gálvez, a Spanish army general who was the new governor of the Spanish-controlled Louisiana Territory, provided weapons and supplies to the American Revolutionaries. He then organized a multicultural force of Spanish soldiers, including Creole militiamen, Indigenous Americans, free Blacks, American volunteers—and a Venezuelan revolutionary named Francisco de Miranda, who opposed the British and supported independence in the Spanish American colonies.

The climactic Battle of Yorktown . . . began on September 28, 1781. The American and French troops closed off Cornwallis’s last escape route and began bombarding the British with cannons. Cornwallis held out for three weeks, but on October 17, 1781—the anniversary of the American victory at Saratoga—he surrendered.

Word help: forged = made · multicultural = made of many cultures · Creole = born in the Americas to Spanish or French families · climactic = the most important, final · Cornwallis = the British general in the South · bombarding = attacking with many shots

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “Setbacks for the British (1777–1781)” (The Spanish Alliance; Yorktown and the Final Campaigns).

Reading HThe Treaty of Paris (1783)

Those negotiations took almost six months. On September 3, 1783, the warring nations signed the Treaty of Paris. Its provisions were surprisingly favorable to the United States. Great Britain recognized the independence of the thirteen former colonies and agreed that the Mississippi River was America’s western boundary, making the new United States larger in size than any European nation . . . Indigenous Americans had no role in the peace negotiations, and they were by far the biggest losers, as the English gave the Americans something that was not theirs to give: vast parcels of ancestral Indigenous lands. Florida, as it turned out, passed back to Spain.

Word help: negotiations = talks to reach an agreement · provisions = the rules in a treaty · favorable = good for · recognized = officially accepted · ancestral = handed down from long-ago family

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “Setbacks for the British (1777–1781)” (The Treaty of Paris (1783)).

An Engine of Change

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 4: Examine how the Revolutionary War was an “engine” for political and social change.

IN MY WORDS: An engine makes things move. What did the war make change?

Reading IAn Engine of Change

Like all major wars, the American fight for independence had unexpected effects on political, economic, and social life. It upset traditional social relationships and affected the lives of people who had long been discriminated against—African Americans, women, and Indigenous Americans. . . . In important ways, then, the Revolution was an engine for political experimentation and social change

American Revolutionaries embraced a republican ideology instead of the aristocratic or monarchical outlook that had long dominated Europe. . . . The United States, however, was technically a representative democracy, in which property-holding White men governed themselves through the concept of republicanism—they elected representatives, or legislators, to make decisions on their behalf.

Word help: experimentation = trying new things · ideology = a set of ideas · aristocratic = ruled by nobles · monarchical = ruled by a king or queen · representatives = people elected to speak for others

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “War as an Engine of Change” (Republican Ideology).

Reading JState Constitutions and Voting

The war for independence thus sparked the crafting of state constitutions to support the new governments, all of which were designed to reflect the principles of the republican ideology and protect the rights of the people. . . . Most of the constitutions also included a bill of rights that protected freedom of speech, trial by jury, and freedom from self-incrimination, while limiting the powers of governors and strengthening the powers of the legislatures.

The new state governments enabled more citizens to participate in self-governance than ever before. Property qualifications for voting, which already allowed an overwhelming majority of White men to vote, were lowered after 1776. . . . As broader voting rights led to a more representative group of government officials, farmers, tradesmen, and shopkeepers were elected to state legislatures.

Word help: sparked = started · constitutions = written plans of government · self-incrimination = being forced to speak against yourself in court · legislatures = groups that make laws · property qualifications = rules that you must own land to vote

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “War as an Engine of Change” (Republican Ideology; Expansion of Political Participation).

Reading KThe Articles of Confederation

Once the colonies had declared their independence in 1776, the new thirteen states needed some form of national government. . . . But it took five years for the squabbling states to ratify the Articles of Confederation (March 1781) . . . The Articles reflected the long-standing fears of monarchy by not allowing for a president or chief executive. The Confederation government also had no national courts and no power to enforce its resolutions and ordinances. The Congress had full power over foreign affairs and disputes between the states; but it could not levy taxes . . . The states were in no mood to create a strong central government.

Word help: squabbling = arguing · ratify = officially approve · chief executive = a leader like a president · enforce = make people obey · levy taxes = collect taxes

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “War as an Engine of Change” (The Articles of Confederation).

Reading LFreedom of Religion

Before the Revolution, Americans tolerated religious dissent; after the Revolution, they insisted on complete freedom of religion as embodied in the principle of separation of church and state. . . . Ten years later, the Virginia Statute of Religious Freedom (written by Thomas Jefferson) declared that “no man shall be compelled to frequent or support any religious worship, place or ministry whatsoever” and “that all men shall be free to profess, and by argument to maintain, their opinions in matters of religion.”

Word help: tolerated = put up with · dissent = disagreement · compelled = forced · frequent = go to often · profess = say openly what you believe

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “War as an Engine of Change” (Freedom of Religion).

Equality and Its Limits

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 5: Compare the impact of the Revolutionary War on people of African descent, women, people of Hispanic descent, and Indigenous Americans.

IN MY WORDS: Did the Revolution bring freedom to every group? Name one group it did not free.

Reading MThe Paradox of Slavery

The sharpest irony of the American Revolution was that Great Britain offered enslaved people more opportunities for freedom than did the United States. In November 1775, the royal governor of Virginia, John Murray (Lord Dunmore), himself the owner of fifty-seven enslaved people, announced that all enslaved people and indentured servants would gain their freedom if they joined “Her Majesty’s troops.”

About 5,000 African Americans fought on the Patriot side, while the British and their allies had 15,000 to 20,000 people of African descent. . . . In the North, which had far fewer enslaved people than the South, the ideals of liberty and freedom led most states to legally abolish slavery, either during the war or shortly afterward . . . But those same ideals had little impact in the southern states.

Word help: irony = the opposite of what you expect · royal governor = the king’s leader of a colony · indentured servants = workers bound to work for years · abolish = end by law

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “Equality and Its Limits” (The Paradox of Slavery; Southern Backlash).

Reading NThe Status of Women

The legal status of women was governed by British common law, which essentially limited their roles to child-rearing and maintaining the household. Women could not vote or hold office, and few had access to formal education.

Yet the Revolution allowed all women, free and enslaved, opportunities to broaden their social roles and to support the armies in various ways. They handled supplies, served as messengers or spies, and worked as “camp followers” who supported soldiers in exchange for daily rations. . . . They responded to wartime needs by managing farms; publishing newspapers; and serving as soldiers, sometimes as spies.

Word help: legal status = rights under the law · common law = old laws based on court decisions · broaden = make wider · rations = daily food for soldiers

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “Equality and Its Limits” (The Status of Women).

Reading OIndigenous Americans Are Besieged

Most Indigenous people sought to remain neutral in the war, but both British and American agents urged Indigenous peoples to fight on their side. . . . During and after the war, the new U.S. government assured its Indigenous allies that it would respect their lands and their rights. But many White Americans used the disruptions of war to destroy and displace Indigenous people. Once the war ended and independence was secured, there was no peace for the Indigenous people and their lands were given away by the Treaty of Paris.

Word help: besieged = surrounded and attacked · neutral = not taking a side · assured = promised · disruptions = disorder, confusion · displace = force out of their homes

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 5, “Equality and Its Limits” (Indigenous Americans Are Besieged).
