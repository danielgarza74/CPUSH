Dilemma 1 of 5 · 1776

1776: Join the Patriots, or Stay Loyal to Britain?

You are there

It is 1776, and you are an adult living in a busy port city in the colonies. Fighting with Britain has started. Some of your neighbors say it is time to break away from the king. Others say that would be treason.

Governors, judges, and other royal officials are mostly on the side of Britain. So are most Anglican ministers. Many small farmers are too. Other people say the only way to protect their liberty is to separate from British control. They want to be citizens who elect their own government, not subjects of a king.

Families and friends are splitting up over this. Your choice may cost you relatives and neighbors. What would you choose?

What really happened

The Revolution turned into a civil war among Americans as well as a war against Britain. Colonists divided into three groups: Patriots, Loyalists, and a middle group that wanted to stay neutral. Even most of the neutral people were eventually forced to choose.

Families were torn apart. Benjamin Franklin’s son William was the royal governor of New Jersey and a Loyalist. His Patriot father removed William from his will and cut off communication.

Many people changed sides during the war. Some switched as many as four or five times. More New York men joined Loyalist regiments than enlisted in the Continental Army.

Source: Norton Ch. 5, “American Society at War” (Loyalists; Patriots).

Dilemma 2 of 5 · 1776

December 1776: Cross the Delaware, or Keep the Army Safe?

You are there

It is December 1776, and you are an officer in General Washington’s army. The Revolution is close to falling apart. Washington has only about 3,000 soldiers left. Sickness and desertion have wrecked the army.

The Americans cannot beat the veteran British soldiers in big battles. Washington has said the war should be defensive. He wants to avoid risky fights and keep his small army alive, so that he can outlast the British. The British have hired thousands of German soldiers, whom Americans call Hessians.

Washington asks his officers whether to stay on the defensive or to risk a surprise attack. He believes the cause needs some “stroke” of good news. What would you advise?

What really happened

Washington chose the attack. On a cold, raw Christmas night in 1776, he led about 2,400 men across the ice-clogged Delaware River into New Jersey. They crossed in forty-foot-long boats and stayed almost silent. Ten of the men were of African descent.

Near sunrise at Trenton, the Americans surprised 1,500 sleeping Hessians. The battle was a rout. Just two Americans were killed and four wounded.

Norton says the victories at Trenton and Princeton saved the cause of independence and shifted the war’s momentum. A fresh wave of Patriots signed up to serve, and Washington regained the confidence of his men.

Source: Norton Ch. 5, “Mobilizing for War” (War: A Stern Teacher; A Desperate Gamble).

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1778

1778: Make an Alliance With France, or Fight On Alone?

You are there

It is early 1778, and you are a member of the Continental Congress. The British have taken Philadelphia, the largest American city, and Congress had to flee. Washington’s army is spending the winter at Valley Forge, hungry and short of coats, shoes, and blankets.

But there is also big news. In October 1777, the Americans surrounded the British army at Saratoga, New York. The British general, Burgoyne, surrendered 5,800 troops, 7,000 muskets, and 42 cannons. William Pitt, a former British prime minister, told Parliament, “You cannot conquer America.”

The victory at Saratoga has made a Treaty of Alliance with France possible. France lost four wars to Britain in the last eighty years and is hungry for revenge. Congress must decide whether to ally with France. What would you choose?

What really happened

The Treaty of Alliance with France was made in 1778. Norton calls the victory at Saratoga a strategic turning point because it made this treaty possible.

In 1779, Spain also joined the war against Britain. Spain was not a great friend of the Americans, but it wanted to hurt Great Britain. Spain’s help was much smaller than France’s.

At Yorktown in 1781, French warships under Admiral de Grasse forced the British navy to leave. That cut off Cornwallis’s army from food and supplies. French troops joined the Americans. On October 17, 1781, Cornwallis surrendered. Some 7,000 British soldiers laid down their weapons.

Source: Norton Ch. 5, “Setbacks for the British (1777–1781)” (The Crucial Alliance with France; Yorktown and the Final Campaigns).

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1786

1786: Make Religion Completely Free, or Keep the Old Way?

You are there

It is 1786, and you are a leader in Virginia’s government. Before the Revolution, Americans tolerated people with other religious beliefs. They did not yet insist on complete freedom. In 1776 Virginia’s Declaration of Rights guaranteed the free exercise of religion.

Now Thomas Jefferson has written a bill for the state. It says that “no man shall be compelled” to attend or support any religious worship. It also says that all men shall be free to hold and argue for their own religious opinions.

Some people say this is the next step in building a free republic. Others want to keep the old way. You must decide how to vote. What would you choose?

What really happened

Virginia’s Statute of Religious Freedom (1786) was written by Thomas Jefferson. It said that “no man shall be compelled” to attend or support any religious worship. It also said that all men shall be free to hold and argue for their own opinions in matters of religion.

Norton says that before the Revolution Americans tolerated religious dissent, but after the Revolution they insisted on complete freedom of religion. This is the principle of separation of church and state.

Norton lists religious freedom, representative democracy, and an expanded right to vote for White men as dramatic changes. But Norton also says the war did not significantly change the social or political status of enslaved Black people or of Indigenous Americans.

Source: Norton Ch. 5, “War as an Engine of Change” (Freedom of Religion).

Dilemma 5 of 5 · 1775-1783

The War Years: Run the Farm at Home, or Support the Army?

You are there

It is during the war, and you are a woman living in the colonies. By law, women cannot vote or hold office. Few women have a chance to go to school. When a woman marries, her property becomes her husband’s.

Many men are away fighting, and the war has changed what women are needed to do. Some women manage farms and shops at home. Some publish newspapers. Others help the army. They handle supplies, carry messages, or work as “camp followers.” Camp followers support soldiers in exchange for daily rations.

At Valley Forge, some twelve-year-old soldiers were with their mothers. The army needs help, and so does your family. What would you choose?

What really happened

Women did both. Norton says the Revolution gave women, free and enslaved, chances to broaden their social roles. They handled supplies, served as messengers or spies, and worked as camp followers. White women in particular managed farms, published newspapers, and sometimes served as soldiers or spies.

But the changes had limits. Norton says women could not vote or hold office, few had a formal education, and a married woman’s property became her husband’s. Norton also says the limits of equality became even more evident after the war.

The war affected other groups in different ways. About 5,000 African Americans fought on the Patriot side. The British and their allies had 15,000 to 20,000 people of African descent. Several hundred thousand enslaved people, mostly in the South, used the war’s disruption to seize their freedom. Indigenous people had no role in the peace talks, and the Treaty of Paris gave away their lands.

Source: Norton Ch. 5, “Equality and Its Limits” (The Status of Women; Southern Backlash; Indigenous Americans Are Besieged).
