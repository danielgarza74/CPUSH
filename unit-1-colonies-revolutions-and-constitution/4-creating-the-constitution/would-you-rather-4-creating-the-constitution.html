1787: Send Delegates to Philadelphia, or Stay Home?

You are there

It is early 1787, and you are a leader in your state’s legislature. The United States is run under the Articles of Confederation. The Articles made a loose alliance of thirteen states. There is only one house of Congress. There is no president and no national court. Each state gets one vote. Rhode Island has 68,000 people and Virginia has more than 747,000, but they have the same voting power.

Congress has no cash and cannot make people pay taxes. Farmers in western Massachusetts have rebelled over debts and taxes, and the state sent 4,400 militiamen to break up their army. George Washington says the country needs a stronger national government to keep people’s lives, liberty, and property safe.

Now Congress has called a special convention to meet in Philadelphia. Its purpose is “revising the Articles of Confederation.” Your state must decide whether to send delegates. Some leaders want a stronger national government. Others want to keep power with the states.

What really happened

The convention met in Philadelphia’s State House, now called Independence Hall. Only Rhode Island refused to take part. All the other states did.

Congress had asked the delegates to revise the Articles. The final draft was a new Constitution. It begins with the words “We the people of the United States.” Norton says the idea that the voters are the source of all authority was the most important political innovation since the Declaration of Independence.

Norton also says the Articles left some good work behind. Congress set the basic rules for dividing western land and for governing new territories. The Northwest Ordinance of 1787 said new territories would someday become equal states.

Source: Norton Ch. 6, “The Confederation Government” and “The Constitution” (The Constitutional Convention).

Dilemma 2 of 5 · 1787

1787: Seats by Population, or One Vote per State?

You are there

It is the summer of 1787, and you are a delegate at the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia. You and the other delegates are writing a plan for a new Congress. The big question is how the states should be represented.

Under the Articles of Confederation, every state had one vote. That meant Rhode Island, with 68,000 people, had the same voting power as Virginia, with more than 747,000.

Delegates from larger states want seats in Congress based on population. Delegates from smaller states want every state to have an equal vote. The debate over this is intense.

What really happened

In mid-July the delegates settled the debate with a deal called the Great Compromise. It used both ideas.

In the House of Representatives, seats are given by population. Larger states win more seats. In the Senate, each state has two members, so every state is equal. At first, state legislatures chose the senators.

Norton says this compromise also built the idea of separation of powers into Congress. A separate agreement, the Three-Fifths Compromise, counted three-fifths of “all other persons” (the enslaved) when seats were divided. It was not part of the choice above.

Source: Norton Ch. 6, “The Constitution” (The Constitutional Convention; The Three-Fifths Compromise) and “The Confederation Government” (A Loose Alliance of States).

1788: Ratify the Constitution, or Say No Without a Bill of Rights?

You are there

It is 1788, and you are a delegate to your state’s convention. Your job is to vote on the new Constitution. The Constitution can take effect only if at least nine states approve it.

The Federalists, led by James Madison and Alexander Hamilton, support it. They have a real plan on the table: the draft itself. They say the country needs the stronger government it creates.

The Anti-Federalists are afraid. They fear the new national government will grow corrupt and tyrannical. George Mason of Virginia and others also point out that the draft has no bill of rights to protect people and states from the national government.

What really happened

On June 21, 1788, New Hampshire became the ninth state to ratify the Constitution. That was the minimum needed for it to take effect.

Madison, Hamilton, and John Jay also wrote eighty-five essays, now called The Federalist Papers. They were printed in New York newspapers in 1787 and 1788 to convince New Yorkers to ratify. Norton says that in Virginia and New York, strong opposition groups were won over by an agreement to add a bill of rights.

Madison, now a congressman, wrote a set of amendments to protect individual rights. By the end of 1791, three-fourths of the states had approved ten of the twelve proposed amendments. We call them the Bill of Rights. Norton adds that, like the Constitution, the Bill of Rights gave no protections to enslaved or Indigenous Americans.

Source: Norton Ch. 6, “The Fight for Ratification” (The Federalist Papers; The States Decide) and “The Federalist Era” (The Bill of Rights).

1791: Create a National Bank, or Say No?

You are there

It is 1791, and you are an adviser to President George Washington. Alexander Hamilton, a top adviser in Washington’s government, wants a national bank, like the Bank of England. He says it would help business among individuals. It would also give the government a safe place to keep its money.

Thomas Jefferson and James Madison lead the opposition. They say the Constitution says nothing about creating a national bank, so the government cannot start one.

The two sides are also two kinds of leaders. Hamilton and his Federalists are strongest in New York and New England. They like cities, industry, and business growth. Jefferson, Madison, and the Republicans are mostly Southerners. They want a nation of small farmers.

What really happened

The Bank of the United States was created in 1791. Norton says it had three main jobs. One was to hold the government’s money and pay its bills. Another was to make loans to the federal government and to state-chartered banks to help the economy grow.

Norton says the vote in Congress was lopsided. It showed the growing political split between North and South.

Norton says the Federalists and the Republicans saw the country in different ways. Federalists wanted a strong central government led by wise leaders who cared about economic growth and national defense. Republicans feared that a strong national government would threaten freedom and states’ rights. They trusted the people.

Source: Norton Ch. 6, “The Federalist Era” (A National Bank; Federalists and Republicans).

Dilemma 5 of 5 · 1795

1795: Accept Jay’s Treaty, or Reject It?

You are there

It is 1795, and you are an adviser to President George Washington. Great Britain and France are at war, and Americans are split. Thomas Jefferson and the Republicans love the French Revolution and its democratic ideals. Alexander Hamilton and John Adams see it as vicious and godless. They side with Great Britain.

Last year, Washington sent John Jay to London to settle the big problems with Britain. Jay agreed that America would not sell products to France for building warships. But the British refused to stop intercepting American ships headed for France and “impressing” their sailors. That means forcing them to serve in the British navy.

In return, Jay won three promises. Britain would give up its six forts on the northwest frontier by 1796. Britain would pay Americans for ships and cargo seized in 1793 and 1794. And U.S. merchants could trade again, in a limited way, with the British West Indies.

Now the terms are public, and many Americans, especially Republicans, are outraged. What would you advise?

What really happened

The treaty was approved. In June 1795 the Senate voted 20 to 10, exactly the two-thirds needed.

Norton says France was angry about the treaty. As the second president, John Adams inherited an undeclared conflict with France that came from that anger.

Norton says these clashing attitudes about Britain and France made the 1790s one of the most fractious periods in American history. Norton calls it an “age of passion.”
