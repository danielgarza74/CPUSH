What really happened

In 1843, the move to Oregon became one of the largest voluntary overland migrations in history. Most settlers traveled in family groups. They left Missouri in late spring and tried to finish the trip by winter.

The trip was as hard as people warned. Wagons broke down, animals died, and cholera killed many travelers. Norton says there was, on average, one grave every eighty yards along the overland trails. By 1845, about 5,000 people had settled in Oregon’s Willamette Valley. The wagon trains also tore through Native American lands and culture.

Source: Norton Ch. 13, “Moving West”.

Dilemma 2 of 5 · May 1846

War with Mexico: Yes or No?

You are there

It is May 1846. President James K. Polk learns that Mexican troops attacked U.S. soldiers along the Rio Grande and killed eleven Americans. He asks Congress to declare war.

But the Rio Grande is about 150 miles south of the Nueces River, which both sides had publicly named as the border. Polk had tried to push the border south to the Rio Grande. Mexico said no.

In the South, where “expansion fever” runs high, many people want war. In the North, many Whigs and abolitionists do not. A Delaware senator says sending troops there was “as much an act of aggression on our part as is a man pointing a pistol at another’s heart.”

What really happened

Congress went along with Polk and authorized 50,000 soldiers. The war was very popular in the South. So many men volunteered that thousands had to be turned back. In the end, 112,000 White men served.

The war lasted from March 1846 to April 1848. More than 13,000 Americans died, and 11,550 of them died from disease. In the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848), Mexico gave up half its territory, and the United States paid $15 million. Opponents kept speaking out. Ulysses S. Grant, who fought in the war, later called it “one of the most unjust wars ever waged by a stronger against a weaker nation.”

Source: Norton Ch. 13, “The Mexican-American War”.

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1846-1848

New Lands: Ban Slavery or Let Settlers Vote?

You are there

It is 1847. The United States is fighting Mexico and may gain a lot of new land. People are asking: will slavery be allowed there?

In August 1846, Congressman David Wilmot of Pennsylvania said slavery should be banned in any land taken from Mexico. Senator Henry Clay said he could not vote to turn free land into slave land, because Mexico had already outlawed slavery there. Some others did not want new slave states gaining power in Congress, or enslaved people competing with free workers.

Senator Lewis Cass of Michigan had a different idea. Let the citizens of each territory “regulate their own internal concerns in their own way.” He called this popular sovereignty. It appealed to many people who wanted to protect states’ rights.

What really happened

President Polk got Wilmot to hold back his amendment, but Wilmot’s idea kept shaping the debate in Congress. Abraham Lincoln later said that in his one term in Congress, he voted forty times to restrict slavery in the new territories. Senator John C. Calhoun offered a third view. He said banning slavery would take property from slaveholders, which he said broke the Fifth Amendment.

A similar idea appeared in the Utah Act of 1850, which let Utah’s legislature decide on slavery, and in the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854. In Kansas, about 5,000 “border ruffians” from Missouri crossed the border on election day, took over polling places, and cast four times as many votes as there were residents. Then in 1857, the Dred Scott decision said Congress could not exclude slavery from a territory. If Congress could not, Norton explains, neither could a territorial government.

Source: Norton Ch. 13, “Slavery in the Territories”.

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1850

The Fugitive Slave Act: Obey or Refuse?

You are there

It is late 1850. You live in a northern city. Congress has passed the Compromise of 1850, and one part of it is a new Fugitive Slave Act.

The law requires the federal government and northern states to help capture freedom seekers and return them to the South. It also requires citizens to help find and catch them. Federal commissioners hear each case. There is no jury, and the person accused cannot give testimony. A commissioner is paid $10 to send someone back to slavery and only $5 to free them.

Ralph Waldo Emerson urges people to break the law. Imagine that a federal officer asks for your help in finding a freedom seeker in your town.

What really happened

Norton calls the Fugitive Slave Act the most controversial part of the Compromise of 1850. By September 20, 1850, President Millard Fillmore had signed all the parts of the Compromise into law. It tried to recover people who had already escaped, and it let slave-traders kidnap free Black people in northern states by claiming they were fugitives.

Abolitionists were furious. Emerson urged people to break the law. In New York, Reverend Jermain Loguen, who had himself escaped slavery, shouted: “I don’t respect this law—I don’t fear it—I won’t obey it! It outlaws me, and I outlaw it.” In late October 1850, two slave-catchers from Georgia arrived in Boston to use the act to recapture William and Ellen Craft.

Source: Norton Ch. 13, “Slavery in the Territories”.

After Lincoln Wins: Secede or Stay?

You are there

It is the winter of 1860. You live in South Carolina. Republican Abraham Lincoln has won the presidency with 39 percent of the popular vote. He carried all eighteen free states and none of the slave states. The Republican Party opposes the extension of slavery into the territories.

South Carolina’s whole congressional delegation has resigned. A convention in Charleston will vote on leaving the Union. Nearly all the delegates own enslaved people. Delegate Thomas Jefferson Withers says the “true question for us all is how shall we sustain African slavery in South Carolina from a series of annoying attacks?”

James L. Petigru, one of the few Unionists in Charleston, says his state “is too small to be a Republic and too large to be an insane asylum.” President James Buchanan says secession is illegal, but he also says he cannot stop it.

What really happened

On December 20, 1860, the South Carolina convention voted unanimously for an Ordinance of Secession. By February 1, 1861, Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas had also left, and seven southern states in all seceded soon after Lincoln’s election. Their ordinances listed several complaints against the federal government, but Norton says the main reason for leaving was their determination to preserve slavery.

The new Confederacy elected Jefferson Davis of Mississippi as its president. President Buchanan, in his last weeks in office, declared secession illegal but said he lacked the power to stop it: “I can do nothing.” Armed southerners surrounded federal forts in the seceded states.

Source: Norton Ch. 13, “The Southern Response”.
