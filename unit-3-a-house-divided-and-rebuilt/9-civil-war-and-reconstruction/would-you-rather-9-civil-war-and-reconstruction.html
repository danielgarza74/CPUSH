Dilemma 1 of 5 · April 1861

After Fort Sumter: Which Side?

You are there

It is April 1861. You live in the Upper South, in a state like Virginia. Confederate forces have just attacked Fort Sumter. Seven Lower South states have already left the Union.

On April 15, President Lincoln asks the “loyal” states for 75,000 militiamen to put down the rebellion. Now everyone in the South has to choose sides.

Many people in your state still feel loyal to the United States. In the mountains, Norton says, White people are poor, enslaved people are scarce, and Union support is strong. Others want to defend their state and their homeland. Even some who do not like secession feel pulled toward their own state.

What really happened

The outbreak of fighting pushed four more slave states into the Confederacy: Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina. Virginia left on April 17, 1861. Delaware stayed in the Union. Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri went through bitter struggles over which side to support.

Lincoln worried most about the border states. “I think to lose Kentucky is nearly the same as to lose the whole game,” he told a friend. To keep Maryland, he had pro-Confederate leaders there arrested. The war split families. Mary Todd Lincoln of Kentucky saw her youngest brother and three half-brothers join the Confederate army. In places with strong Union feeling, such as eastern Tennessee and western Virginia, the war became brutal and personal.

Source: Norton Ch. 14, “Mobilizing Forces in the North and South,” Choosing Sides, pp. 572-573.

Dilemma 2 of 5 · Summer 1862

President Lincoln: Free the Enslaved or Wait?

You are there

It is the summer of 1862. You are President Abraham Lincoln. The war is more than a year old. Many people had hoped it would be short.

So far, you have said the war is about saving the Union, not ending slavery. But tens of thousands of enslaved people have already run away to Union army camps. They hurt the Confederate war effort by leaving, and they help the Union army by working as laborers.

Some people tell you to wait. The border slave states of Delaware, Kentucky, Maryland, and Missouri are still in the Union, and you worry about losing them. You also doubt that the president has the constitutional power to end slavery. Your Secretary of State, William Seward, agrees with you on emancipation but says to wait for a Union victory so it does not look like desperation.

What really happened

Lincoln chose to act after the Battle of Antietam. On September 22, 1862, he issued the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation. It warned that if Confederate leaders did not stop fighting, all enslaved people under their control would be made “forever free” on January 1, 1863. Lincoln called it a “military necessity.” It did not free enslaved people in the four border states or in Confederate areas already held by Union forces. When he signed the final proclamation in January, he added that it was also “an act of justice.”

Reactions were mixed. The New York Times called it a far reaching document. Democrats called it dictatorial and unconstitutional. Thousands of White Union soldiers deserted, saying they had not enlisted to free enslaved people, and Democrats won twenty-eight Republican seats in Congress. Lincoln answered his critics: “Fight you, then, exclusively to save the Union.” The Thirteenth Amendment, adopted December 18, 1865, later ended slavery everywhere in the United States.

Source: Norton Ch. 14, “Emancipation,” pp. 590-595.

Dilemma 3 of 5 · May 1863

Free Black Man in the North: Enlist or Stay Out?

You are there

It is May 1863. You are a free Black man living in the North. The Emancipation Proclamation has just gone into effect. The Union army wants to recruit Black men in large numbers for the first time.

On May 22, the War Department creates the Bureau of Colored Troops to recruit free Black men and formerly enslaved people. Frederick Douglass says, “This is our chance, and woe betide us if we fail to embrace it.”

But you have heard the facts. Black soldiers will serve in all-Black units led by White officers. They will be paid $7 a month, while White recruits get $16. They will not get the enlistment bonus that White recruits get. At first, Black soldiers are not allowed in combat. And the war is deadly for every soldier.

What really happened

More than 180,000 Black men enlisted in the Union army. Most were formerly enslaved, and 80 percent came from Southern states. In the navy, African Americans made up about a fourth of all enlistments. Norton calls their recruitment “the most revolutionary episode of the war.”

The need to win the war soon put Black soldiers into combat. By late spring 1863, a White Union private wrote that they “fight like the Devil.” Many enlisted to win rights for their people. Jacob Christy, a free Black man from Pennsylvania, wrote in 1864 that he would die “a trying for our rights.” The risks were real. In April 1864, at Fort Pillow, Confederate troops killed about 300 surrendering Union soldiers, most of them African Americans.

Source: Norton Ch. 14, “Emancipation,” Black Soldiers and Sailors, p. 597.

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1866-1867

Rebuilding the South: Johnson’s Plan or Congress’s Plan?

You are there

It is 1866. The Civil War is over, and President Lincoln has been assassinated. The new president, Andrew Johnson, has a plan to bring the Southern states back into the Union. Congress has to decide whether to go along with it.

Johnson’s plan is lenient. Each Southern state must hold a convention and ratify the Thirteenth Amendment, which ended slavery. Many former Confederates can get a pardon. Johnson says Congress cannot grant citizenship to Black people and that its laws trespass on states’ rights.

But the Southern states have passed Black codes that limit the lives of formerly enslaved people. In Memphis, White mobs attack Black neighborhoods, and forty-six African Americans are killed. Radical Republicans, led by Thaddeus Stevens and Charles Sumner, say Johnson’s plan lets White supremacists off easy. They want a stronger plan: a Fourteenth Amendment on citizenship and equal protection, and army control of the South until states follow new rules.

What really happened

Congress chose the second plan. It passed the Civil Rights Act over Johnson’s veto, the first time Congress overrode a presidential veto of a major bill. It passed the Fourteenth Amendment in 1866, and it was ratified in 1868. In the elections of 1866, Radical Republicans won more than two-thirds of each house. In March 1867, Congress passed the Military Reconstruction Act over Johnson’s vetoes. It put ten Southern states under army control in five military districts. Each state had to write a new constitution that let all adult men vote, Black or White, and ratify the Fourteenth Amendment.

Norton says this was the most sweeping peacetime legislation in American history to that point. Congress later impeached Johnson in 1868. The Senate fell one vote short of removing him, and he stayed in office. Reconstruction did not last, however. Federal troops left the South in 1877.

Source: Norton Ch. 15, “Battles over Political Reconstruction,” Johnson’s Reconstruction Plan, pp. 637-638.

Dilemma 5 of 5 · February 1877

A Disputed Election: Take the Deal or Keep Fighting?

You are there

It is late February 1877. Months ago, the country voted for president. Democrat Samuel Tilden won almost 300,000 more votes than Republican Rutherford B. Hayes. But Tilden is one electoral vote short of winning.

The election now depends on 19 disputed electoral votes from Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina. Republican-run election boards in those states said Hayes won narrowly, and Democrats are challenging the results. Democrats need only one of the 19 votes. Republicans need all nineteen. Weeks have passed with no answer, and Congress has named a commission to settle it.

Now some leaders are talking about a private deal. Hayes would become president, and he would remove the last federal troops from the South. Federal troops still in the South help protect the civil rights of Black Southerners. In 1876, Frederick Douglass warned that freedom means little if Black people are “subject to the slaveholder’s shotgun.” Others fear that a long fight could bring a new civil war.

What really happened

The deal went ahead. On February 26, powerful Ohio Republicans and Southern Democrats made a private, unwritten bargain at Wormley’s Hotel in Washington, D.C. This is called the Compromise of 1877. On March 1, an electoral commission voted 8-7 for Hayes. The next day the House declared Hayes president by 185 electoral votes to 184. Tilden decided not to protest. His campaign manager said they preferred “four years of Hayes’s administration to four years of civil war.”

Hayes then ordered U.S. soldiers in the South Carolina statehouse back to their barracks, and the state’s Republican government soon collapsed. Congress also stopped paying for federal troops in the South. Norton says that without federal troops, African Americans could not keep their newly won civil rights. White supremacists found ways to stop Black people from voting, and Norton sums it up this way: the North had won the Civil War but lost the peace.

Source: Norton Ch. 15, “The Grant Administration,” The Contested Election of 1876, pp. 668-669.
