Moving West and Texas

CORE OBJECTIVE

1: Explain how, why, and where Americans moved west, and who already lived in the lands they wanted.

IN MY WORDS: Who moved west, and whose land was it already?

Reading AWho Moved West, and Whose Land Was It?

The idea of a “manifest destiny” implied that the United States had a God-given mission to extend its Christian republic and capitalist civilization from the Atlantic to the Pacific—and beyond. It offered a powerful, if self-serving, religious justification for expansion at the expense of Indigenous and Hispanic people, as well as the Spaniards and the British, all of whom resided in the western territories that Americans coveted. . . .

Most of the new western settlers were American-born Whites from the Upper South and the Midwest. . . . What spurred the migration westward was the nation’s continuing population explosion and the widespread desire for land. . . .

In 1840, when the massive migration across the Great Plains began, more than 325,000 Indigenous people inhabited the vast area west of the Mississippi River. They represented more than 200 nations, each with its own language, religion, cultural practices, and system of governance. . . .

By 1845, there were perhaps 800 White American settlers in California, along with approximately 10,000 Californios (Hispanic Californians) and 150,000 Indigenous people.

Word help: implied = suggested · self-serving = helping yourself · at the expense of = making someone else pay · coveted = wanted badly · inhabited = lived in · Californios = Hispanic Californians

Norton, Ch. 13, “Moving West” (opening; The Western Frontier; Indigenous Plains Peoples; John Sutter). Printed book pp. 514–522.

Reading BTexas and Slavery

By 1823, 3,000 Americans (or Texians, also called Anglos because they spoke English) were living in Texas. Most had migrated from southern states . . . . They dreamed of great cotton wealth to be made in Texas—and they forced enslaved people to move with them. . . .

Mexican officials became so concerned about the Americans living in Texas that in 1830 they outlawed immigration from the United States . . . . In a letter to his cousin in 1835, Stephen Austin left no doubt about his plans: “It is very evident that Texas should be effectually, and fully, Americanized. . . . Texas must be a slave country. It is no longer a matter of doubt.”

In 1836, the Lone Star Republic, as Texians called their new nation, legalized slavery and banned free Black people. . . . On March 1, 1845, Tyler signed the resolution admitting Texas to the Union as the fifteenth slave state.

Word help: Texians = Americans who settled in Texas · annexation = adding land to a country · legalized = made legal

Norton, Ch. 13, “Moving West” (American Settlements in Texas; A Southbound Underground Railroad; The Lone Star Republic; The State of Texas). Printed book pp. 523–530.

War with Mexico and Its Price

CORE OBJECTIVE

2: Examine the causes and results of the Mexican-American War, including its impact on Californios and Indigenous Californians.

IN MY WORDS: What did the United States win, and who paid the price?

Reading CWhy War with Mexico?

On March 6, 1845, two days after James K. Polk became president, the Mexican government broke off relations with the United States to protest the annexation of Texas. Polk wanted a war with Mexico, which would likely end with the U.S. acquisition of California and New Mexico—but he did not want Americans to fire the first shot. So he sent several thousand troops, under General Zachary Taylor, to the Rio Grande. That was some 150 miles south of the Nueces River, which both sides had publicly recognized as the southern border . . . .

Most Whigs across the North, including a young Illinois congressman named Abraham Lincoln, also opposed the war, stressing that President Polk had maneuvered the Mexicans into attacking.

Word help: acquisition = getting something · maneuvered = tricked or pushed someone into doing something

Norton, Ch. 13, “The Mexican-American War” (opening; Opposition to the War). Printed book pp. 532–533.

Reading DThe Treaty and California

By the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed in February 1848, a humiliated Mexico gave up half its entire territory . . . . The treaty guaranteed the 150,000 Indigenous people and the 80,000 Mexicans living in the ceded territories that they could keep their property, receive U.S. citizenship, and retain their Catholic religion. . . . Over time, however, as Americans streamed in, the ruthless among them cheated and killed Mexican-born residents and Native people. . . .

For Indigenous people in California, however, the gold rush was a disaster. In 1848, there were 150,000 in the territory, ten times more than Whites; by 1870, there were only 30,000 (an 80 percent decrease). . . . In 1850, White miners convinced the new state legislature to pass a Foreign Miners’ Tax that drove most Mexicans from the diggings.

Word help: humiliated = embarrassed and defeated · ceded = given up · retain = keep · ruthless = cruel, without pity · diggings = places where miners dug for gold

Norton, Ch. 13, “The Mexican-American War” (The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo); “Slavery in the Territories” (Conquering Indigenous Americans and Mexican Americans). Printed book pp. 536–542.

Compromises That Failed

CORE OBJECTIVE

3: Describe how the national government tried to settle slavery in the western territories, and why each attempt failed.

IN MY WORDS: How did Congress try to settle slavery in the West, and why didn't it work?

Reading EWho Decides About Slavery in New Land?

On August 8, 1846, only three months into the Mexican-American War, an obscure Democratic congressman from Pennsylvania, David Wilmot, delivered a speech to the House of Representatives in which he proposed that if any Mexican territory should be acquired, slavery would be banned there. . . . The House of Representatives approved the Wilmot Proviso, but the Senate balked. . . .

Senator Lewis Cass of Michigan suggested a different solution: that the citizens of each territory “regulate their own internal concerns in their own way” and decide for themselves whether slavery would be legal. This would take the issue out of Congress and put it in the hands of those directly affected. Popular sovereignty, as Cass called his idea, appealed to many who were eager to protect states’ rights.

Word help: obscure = not well known · acquired = gained · balked = refused to go along · regulate = control · “states’ rights” = the idea that states, not the national government, should decide

Norton, Ch. 13, “Slavery in the Territories” (The Wilmot Proviso; Popular Sovereignty). Printed book pp. 538–539.

Two answers to one question: Who would decide about slavery in the new land under the Wilmot Proviso? Who would decide under popular sovereignty?

Reading FThe Fugitive Slave Act (1850)

The Fugitive Slave Act was the most controversial element of the Compromise of 1850. . . . The law even enabled slave-traders to kidnap free Blacks in northern “free” states, claiming that they were fugitive slaves; and it required citizens to help locate and capture any freedom seekers who had successfully escaped. In addition, the act dispatched federal commissioners to hear disputes between freedom seekers and slave-catchers . . . . There was no jury, no testimony was allowed from the fugitives, and the commissioners themselves were paid $10 for returning runaways to slavery—but only $5 to free them. . . .

Soon, several northern states countered the Fugitive Slave Act by passing what were called personal liberty laws. Some of these state laws allowed jury trials for freedom seekers who had been captured and others prohibited state authorities from cooperating with slave catchers.

Word help: controversial = causing strong arguments · freedom seekers = people escaping slavery · commissioners = officials who decided cases · countered = fought back against · prohibited = did not allow

Norton, Ch. 13, “Slavery in the Territories” (The Fugitive Slave Act). Printed book pp. 548–549.

Reading GBleeding Kansas

While Nebraska would become a free state, Kansas was up for grabs. According to the Kansas-Nebraska Act, the residents of the Kansas Territory, not Congress, would decide whether to allow slavery. . . .

On Election Day, 5,000 “border ruffians” from Missouri crossed into Kansas. They seized polling places, illegally elected pro-slavery legislators (casting four times as many votes as there were residents) . . . . By 1856, a free-state “governor” and “legislature” were operating in Topeka; thus, there were two illegal governments in the Kansas Territory. Soon the political conflict mushroomed into a local civil war, which journalists called Bleeding Kansas. . . . By the end of 1856, about 200 settlers had been killed in Bleeding Kansas.

Word help: up for grabs = not decided yet · “border ruffians” = armed men from Missouri · polling places = where people vote · mushroomed = grew fast

Norton, Ch. 13, “The Emergence of the Republican Party” (Bleeding Kansas; The Pottawatomie Massacre). Printed book pp. 554–555.

A House Divided

CORE OBJECTIVES

4: Analyze how Dred Scott and the fight over Kansas divided the nation and led to Lincoln's election.

5: Explain why seven southern states seceded, and how Lincoln answered the claim that a state could leave the Union.

IN MY WORDS: How did the nation break apart?

Reading HDred Scott v. Sandford (1857)

Chief Justice Roger B. Taney of Maryland, a devoted supporter of the South and of slavery, wrote the majority opinion. He ruled that Scott lacked legal standing because, like all enslaved people, he was property, not a human being. He was not a U.S. citizen and could never become one, nor was he eligible to file suit in a court. . . .

The Dred Scott case directly challenged the concept of popular sovereignty. If Congress could not exclude slavery from a territory, as Taney argued, then neither could a territorial government created by an act of Congress. Suddenly, all the West—and the North—was open to slavery.

Word help: majority opinion = the court’s official ruling · legal standing = the right to sue in court · eligible = allowed · exclude = keep out

Norton, Ch. 13, “The Emergence of the Republican Party” (The Dred Scott Case). Printed book p. 558.

Source I“A House Divided” (Primary Source)

On June 16, 1858, Abraham Lincoln accepted the Illinois Republican Party’s nomination for the U.S. Senate, the race he ran against Stephen Douglas. Norton quotes this speech in Chapter 13. (“A house divided against itself cannot stand” is a line from the Bible.)

“‘A house divided against itself cannot stand.’ I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free. I do not expect the Union to be dissolved—I do not expect the house to fall—but I do expect it will cease to be divided. It will become all one thing, or all the other. Either the opponents of slavery, will arrest the further spread of it, and place it where the public mind shall rest in the belief that it is in course of ultimate extinction; or its advocates will push it forward till it shall become alike lawful in all the States, old as well as new—North as well as South.”

Word help: endure = last · dissolved = broken up · cease = stop · arrest = stop · ultimate extinction = ending for good · advocates = supporters · alike lawful = legal in the same way

Abraham Lincoln, “House Divided” speech, Springfield, Illinois, June 16, 1858. Public-domain text (National Park Service, Lincoln Home National Historic Site). Quoted in Norton, Ch. 13, Douglas versus Lincoln (1858), pp. 560–561.

Reading JSecession

Meeting in Charleston on December 20, 1860, the special convention unanimously approved an Ordinance of Secession separating South Carolina from the United States. . . .

By February 1, 1861, the other states of the Lower South—Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas—had also seceded. Although their secession ordinances mentioned various grievances against the federal government, they made clear that the primary reason for leaving the Union was their determination to preserve slavery. . . .

Lincoln insisted that “the Union of these States is perpetual,” and therefore the notion of a Confederate nation was a fiction. No state, he stressed, “can lawfully get out of the Union.”

Word help: unanimously = everyone voted yes · ordinance = a law · secede = leave the Union · grievances = complaints · perpetual = lasting forever · lawfully = legally

Norton, Ch. 13, “The Southern Response” (South Carolina secedes; Secession of the Lower South; Lincoln’s Inauguration). Printed book pp. 565–567.
