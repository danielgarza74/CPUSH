1925: Buy Now, Pay Later?

You are there

It is 1925. The economy is booming. Jobs are plentiful, and wage workers are earning more. Your family wants a car.

Henry Ford’s Model T cost $850 when it first appeared in 1908. By 1924, Ford sold the same car for $290.

Stores now offer layaway plans and installment buying. Ads say “buy now, pay later.” Business owners call the new consumer culture a marvel of modern life. Critics say it threatens old virtues like hard work, plain living, and careful money management.

What really happened

Many Americans chose to buy now and pay later. Norton says consumer debt almost tripled during the 1920s. Advertising kept driving demand anyway.

Car ownership grew fast. More than 8 million cars were registered in 1920. By 1929, there were more than 23 million. The auto industry created tens of thousands of new jobs.

But Norton says the new conveniences of modern life were enjoyed mainly by the urban middle and upper classes. Rural and working-class Americans had to wait many more years.

Source: Norton Ch. 22, “A ‘New Era’ of Consumption,” A Growing Consumer Culture and The Car Culture, pp. 949-952.

A Young Woman in 1925: New Ways or Old Ways?

You are there

It is 1925. You are a young woman in a big American city. Women have won the right to vote.

What really happened

Both kinds of women were real. Young women who tried the new freedoms got a lot of attention. By 1927, skirts were at the knee. In 1919, they had been six inches above the ground. Their “carefree version of feminism” also aroused furious criticism.

But Norton says most women in the 1920s were not rebels like flappers. Most people still led traditional lives. Fewer college-educated women pursued careers outside the home. Most women who worked outside the home had unskilled, low-paying jobs. Only 4 percent of working women were salaried professionals.

Source: Norton Ch. 22, “The ‘Jazz Age,’” Women in the 1920s, p. 957, and Not-So-‘New’ Women, p. 959.

1913: The Armory Show and Modern Art

You are there

It is 1913. You are in New York City. A huge art show called the Armory Show has opened. It brings modern art from Europe to Americans.

Modernism began in European capitals like Paris and London in the 1890s. Now it has reached New York and Chicago. Modern artists break the old rules on purpose. One modernist painter said, “Art is meant to disturb.”

Many visitors are upset. A well-known art critic says modernism “is nothing else than the total destruction of the art of painting.”

What really happened

Americans did not agree. Norton calls the Armory Show the most controversial event in the history of American art. For many people who toured it, modern art became the thing they “loved to hate.”

Modernism kept spreading anyway. Norton says it reflected a sense that Western civilization was going through bewildering changes. New technology, new transportation and communication, and new scientific discoveries were changing everyday life. The poet Ezra Pound gave modernism its slogan: “Make It New!”

By the 1920s, modernists and their traditional critics were clashing in passionate defense of their views. The author Willa Cather said, “The world broke in two in 1922 or thereabouts.”

Source: Norton Ch. 22, “The Modernist Revolt,” pp. 965-969.

July 1925: The Scopes Trial

You are there

It is July 1925. You are in Dayton, Tennessee, a small town. Tennessee has passed a law that bans the teaching of evolution in public schools and colleges.

Town leaders wanted publicity. They asked John T. Scopes, a 24-year-old high-school science teacher, to be a test case. He taught from a textbook on evolution, and police arrested him. Hundreds of reporters have come to town for the trial.

William Jennings Bryan believes the Bible is literally true. He has volunteered to help the prosecution. He says the trial is about a state’s right to decide what is taught in its public schools. Clarence Darrow, a famous trial lawyer, has offered to defend Scopes and the theory of evolution. Darrow says, “Scopes is not on trial. Civilization is on trial.”

Many Americans are watching. Most American Christians fall somewhere between the two sides.

What really happened

The trial began on July 10, 1925, and lasted twelve days. The judge ruled that the only question for the jury was whether Scopes had taught evolution. No one denied that he had. The jury found Scopes guilty.

The Tennessee Supreme Court upheld the anti-evolution law. But it waived Scopes’s $100 fine on a technicality. Both sides claimed victory.

Five days after the trial ended, Bryan died. Norton says the Scopes Trial only sharpened the national debate between fundamentalism and evolution. That debate continues today.

Source: Norton Ch. 22, “The Reactionary Twenties,” Fundamentalism and The Scopes Trial, pp. 976-979.

1921: Cut Taxes or Keep Regulating Business?

You are there

It is 1921. Warren Harding has won the presidency by a wide margin, 16 million votes to 9 million for James Cox. He promised “normalcy.” Republicans have also gained seats in both houses of Congress.

Before the war, progressives pushed for tighter rules on business. They are still strong in state and local governments, where they work on public schools, public health, and social-welfare programs. But in Washington, conservative Republicans are back in power.

Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon wants to cut federal spending, lower tax rates, and raise tariffs on imported goods. This year, the top income tax rate is 73 percent. He says these steps will help the economy grow.

Progressives say the national government should stay active and keep regulating business.

What really happened

In the 1920s, the national government chose A. Mellon convinced Congress to cut the top income tax rate from 73 percent in 1921 to 24 percent by 1929. Government spending and the national debt fell. Unemployment dropped to 2.4 percent in 1923. President Coolidge later said, “The chief business of the American people is business.”

Harding and his team also dismantled or neutralized many progressive regulatory laws and agencies. The Court led by Taft struck down a federal child-labor law and a minimum-wage law for women.

Unions lost ground. Employers asked new workers to sign “yellow-dog” contracts, which forced them to promise not to join a union. Union membership fell from about 5 million in 1920 to 3.5 million in 1929. Norton says the “new economy” was not helping enough working-class Americans to last.

Source: Norton Ch. 22, “Republican Resurgence,” pp. 983-987 and 991.
