May 1915: The Lusitania Is Sunk

You are there

It is May 1915. Europe has been at war since 1914. The United States is neutral. Most Americans want to stay out of the fighting.

Germany has warned that ships near the British Isles may be attacked. President Wilson has warned Germany that he will hold it to “strict accountability” for lost lives and property.

On May 7, a German submarine fires one torpedo at the British passenger ship Lusitania. The ship sinks off the coast of Ireland. A total of 1,198 people die. 128 of them are Americans. Fifty of the dead are infants.

Former president Theodore Roosevelt says the sinking calls for an immediate declaration of war. President Wilson asks for patience. He says, “There is such a thing as a man being too proud to fight.”

What really happened

Wilson chose patience. On May 13, Secretary of State William Jennings Bryan demanded that Germany stop unrestricted submarine warfare, apologize, and pay the families of those killed. Germany said the ship carried rifles, ammunition, and Canadian soldiers. Norton says that was true. Wilson still dismissed the German claims. Bryan felt Wilson favored Britain, and he resigned in protest.

Germany made two pledges to protect passenger ships, the Arabic Pledge and the Sussex Pledge. But on January 31, 1917, German leaders ordered unrestricted submarine warfare to start again.

On April 2, 1917, Wilson asked Congress to declare war. The Senate voted 82 to 6. The House voted 373 to 50. Wilson signed the war resolution on April 6. The United States had stayed officially neutral for thirty months.

Source: Norton Ch. 21, “An Uneasy Neutrality,” Neutral Rights and Submarine Attacks through Wilson Declares War, pp. 912-915.

Dilemma 2 of 5 · 1916

A Black Family in the South: Move North or Stay?

You are there

It is 1916. You are part of a Black family in the South. Many Black families there are sharecroppers. As late as 1900, 92 percent of Black Americans lived in the South.

The Great War in Europe has made war industries in northern cities grow fast. They need new workers. Trains are heading north. Many people call the North the “promised land.” They hear that jobs pay better there, schools are better, and Black people can speak more freely and vote.

Others warn that the move is risky. The writer Richard Wright said that “never in history” has “a more utterly unprepared folk wanted to go to the city.” In northern cities, many Black newcomers are strangers. Some are cheated by white landlords, realtors, and bankers. Some are forced into poor, segregated housing and paid less than white workers. Some clash with Irish and Italian neighbors who fear losing their jobs.

What really happened

The first great wave of Black migrants left the South in 1915-1916. The departures continued through the 1920s. Almost a million African Americans, mostly sharecroppers and their families, boarded trains north. Most went to big cities such as New York, Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, Washington, D.C., and Philadelphia. This movement is called the Great Migration.

In the North, Black voters became a “swing vote” in big states, which gave African Americans more political power than before. Over time, the newcomers built new lives, churches, communities, and cultural activities. But the problems were real too. Norton says northern discrimination, bad though it was, still paled beside the injustices of the segregated South. That is why millions went.

In 1919, about 50,000 African Americans, mostly migrants from the South, were living in Chicago. In July, five days of rioting there killed 38 people and injured 537. The Red Summer mocked the dreams of African Americans who had come north seeking a promised land.

Source: Norton Ch. 21, “Mobilizing for War,” The Great Migration, pp. 919-920, and “Stumbling from War to Peace,” Race Riots, pp. 940-941.

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1918

1918: Where Should American Soldiers Go?

You are there

It is 1918. The United States is in the Great War. The army was small when the United States joined the war, and it is still training and shipping men overseas. There are not enough troop ships.

French leader Georges Clemenceau has begged the Americans to hurry. He fears “a terrible blow” from Germany on the Western Front, the battle line in France and Belgium. The Allies said they needed at least one million U.S. soldiers.

In Russia, the Communist Bolsheviks have taken power. Their leader, Lenin, wants Russia out of the war. If Russia leaves, Germany can send more soldiers west. A civil war has started in Russia. The United States and its allies want to help the anti-Communist Russian forces.

President Wilson could send American soldiers to Siberia, in far eastern Russia, to help those forces. But every soldier sent to Siberia is a soldier not sent to France.

What really happened

Wilson did both. He sent 20,000 American soldiers to Siberia to support the anti-Communist forces. The effort was not successful. Lenin signed a peace treaty with Germany, the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, on March 3, 1918.

In March 1918, Germany began a series of spring attacks in France and Belgium. By May, German armies were within fifty miles of Paris. In June, U.S. Marines helped the French drive the Germans back at Belleau Wood.

On September 26, 1918, American troops joined British and French armies in a drive toward Sedan, France. About 1.2 million U.S. soldiers took part, including about 180,000 African Americans. It was the largest American action of the war. There were 117,000 U.S. casualties, including 26,000 dead. The German general Erich Ludendorff wrote that “America became the decisive power in the war.”

Summer 1919: Wilson’s Treaty in the Senate

You are there

It is the summer of 1919. The Great War is over. President Wilson has come home from Paris with the Treaty of Versailles. It includes his plan for a League of Nations. You are a senator. The treaty needs approval from at least two-thirds of the Senate.

Wilson says the League is “the heart” of the peace plan. He says that without it there will be “another world war.” He asks the Senate, “Dare we reject it and break the heart of the world?”

Many Republican senators have doubts. Henry Cabot Lodge says the League could send U.S. troops to foreign conflicts without Congress approving. He says that would break the Constitution. A group of 16 senators will not accept the League at all. Lodge’s group would approve the treaty if there were changes that limit American part in the League. The most important change would keep Congress in charge of deciding whether to join a League war.

Wilson has told a French diplomat, “The Senate must take its medicine.” His close adviser, Colonel Edward House, urges him to meet the Senate in a friendly spirit. House says American civilization was “built on compromise.”

What really happened

Wilson refused any changes. In September 1919, he went on a three-week train tour to speak to the voters. On September 25 he collapsed in Pueblo, Colorado. Days after he returned to Washington, he had a stroke. It left him paralyzed on his left side and partly blind.

Lodge pushed fourteen changes through the Senate. Wilson rejected all of them. In 1920, the Senate voted on Lodge’s revised treaty. It lost, 39 in favor and 55 against. The original treaty also lost, 38 in favor and 53 against.

The United States never approved the treaty. The League of Nations was created without American participation. A Connecticut senator said that Wilson’s refusal to compromise meant he had “strangled his own child.”

Source: Norton Ch. 21, “The Politics of Peace,” The League of Nations through The Treaty under Attack, pp. 930-937.

September 1919: The Boston Police Strike

You are there

It is September 1919. The war is over, but life at home is not calm. Prices have jumped. Many workers want higher wages. In 1919, more than 4 million workers take part in 3,600 strikes.

Most workers want higher pay and shorter workweeks. But some business leaders say strikes are a step toward a Bolshevik revolution like the one in Russia. Norton says that charge was greatly exaggerated. Fewer than 70,000 people in the country belonged to the Communist Party.

On September 9, police in Boston go on strike. You are Calvin Coolidge, the governor of Massachusetts. You send in the National Guard to keep order. After four panicky days of looting, the striking police offer to come back to work.

Labor leaders say the police should get their jobs back. Others say police must protect the public, and that no one has a right to strike against public safety.

What really happened

Coolidge ordered that all of the striking police be fired. When labor leaders asked for their jobs back, he answered, “There is no right to strike against the public safety by anybody, anywhere, any time.” To some Americans, those tough words made him an instant national hero.

Norton calls the Boston strike the most controversial labor dispute of 1919. It came in a year of inflation, strikes, race riots, and fear of Communists that led to the First Red Scare.

Source: Norton Ch. 21, “Stumbling from War to Peace,” Economic Turbulence, pp. 939-940.
