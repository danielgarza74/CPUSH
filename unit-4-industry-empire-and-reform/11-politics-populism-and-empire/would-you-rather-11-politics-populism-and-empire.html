The Election of 1884: Stay Loyal or Break Away?

You are there

It is 1884. You are a Republican voter. Your family has voted Republican for years. In these years, party loyalty is very strong. Norton says it often lasts through many generations of a family.

Your party has chosen James G. Blaine of Maine to run for president. He gives electrifying speeches and knows how to make backroom deals. But newspapers say the Mulligan letters show that he secretly sold his votes as Speaker of the House to help a railroad company. There is no proof that he committed a crime, but the evidence looks strong.

Some Republicans refuse to back him. They want honest government. They see this election as a “moral rather than political” contest. Party regulars scorn them as the “good-government” crowd. An editor jokingly calls them Mugwumps. Most are professors, editors, and writers. Their most famous member is Mark Twain.

The Democrats have chosen Grover Cleveland, the governor of New York. He is known for his honesty. As governor, he fought to clean up New York City’s corrupt Tammany Hall ring. Cleveland also has personal troubles that are in the news.

What really happened

The race was very close. Cleveland won the Electoral College, 219 to 182. His lead in the popular vote was fewer than 30,000 votes out of 10 million. Norton says that two mistakes by Blaine’s campaign near the end may have tipped the election. Republicans said Democrats had paid many New York voters. Either way, a Democrat was back in the White House for the first time since the Civil War.

President Cleveland tried to keep the job-giving system (patronage) out of government. But during his two terms, about two-thirds of the 120,000 federal jobs still went to Democrats.

Dilemma 2 of 5 · 1890

Farm Leaders in 1890: A New Party or the Old One?

You are there

It is 1890. You are a leader in a Farmers’ Alliance. Crop prices keep falling, and farmers are deep in debt. Southern cotton sold for 31 cents a pound in 1866. By 1886, it sold for 9 cents. Many small farmers borrow money from local banks and merchants at high interest rates.

In 1887, Alliance leaders tried a plan for farmers to help themselves. Farmers pooled their money to borrow and to buy supplies together. But the plan died when banks refused to lend them money.

Now many farm leaders think they need political power. They are frustrated because few leading Democrats or Republicans support their cause.

Some Alliances want to start new political parties. In 1890, Nebraska farmers started the People’s Independent Party. In Colorado, farmers joined miners and railroad workers to form the Independent Party.

Others hesitate to leave their old party. Across the South, White Alliance members do not want to leave the Democratic Party. They want to take control of it instead.

What really happened

In 1890, both paths worked better than anyone expected. Third parties took control of one house of the Kansas legislature and both houses in Nebraska. In the South, the Alliance elected four supporters as Democratic governors and forty-four as members of Congress. Several became U.S. senators, and pro-Alliance candidates controlled seven state legislatures.

In 1892, Alliance leaders and others met in Omaha, Nebraska, and formed the People’s Party, also called the Populists. Their candidate, James B. Weaver, won more than 1 million votes and carried four states: Colorado, Kansas, Nevada, and Idaho. Grover Cleveland won the presidency.

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1898

President McKinley: Wait or Go to War?

You are there

It is early 1898. You are President William McKinley. Cubans have been fighting for independence from Spain since 1895. During the Cuban War for Independence (1895-1898), at least 95,000 Cubans, mostly peasants, died of wounds, disease, and starvation in Spanish detention camps.

American newspapers print shocking stories about Spain in Cuba. Some are real and some are invented. This is called yellow journalism. American companies also have money invested in Cuban sugar and mining.

On February 15, the battleship Maine explodes and sinks in Havana Harbor. Two hundred sixty-six sailors die. No one has shown evidence of what caused it. But many newspapers blame Spain, and people cry, “Remember the Maine!”

Some leaders want war now. Theodore Roosevelt, a Navy official, says you have “no more backbone than a chocolate éclair.” Senator Henry Cabot Lodge, your closest friend, is another “jingoist” (a war-loving patriot).

You do not want to be rushed. Norton says neither country wants a war. In the weeks after the Maine, Spain agrees to every major American demand about its rule over Cuba.

What really happened

On April 11, McKinley asked Congress for the authority to use the armed forces to end the fighting in Cuba. Congress demanded that Spanish troops leave. Spain cut off relations and declared war on April 24, 1898. The next day, Congress declared war too. The Teller Amendment said the United States did not plan to annex (take over) Cuba.

Norton says McKinley went to war reluctantly. The war lasted only 114 days. On December 10, 1898, the Treaty of Paris made Cuba independent, and the United States took Puerto Rico and Guam. Years later, the sinking of the Maine was ruled an accident caused by a coal explosion on the ship.

Source: Norton Ch. 19, “The Spanish-American War,” The Cuban Independence Movement and The Political Path to War, pp. 824-827.

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1899

The Treaty of Paris: Keep the Philippines or Not?

You are there

It is early 1899. You are a U.S. senator. The United States has beaten Spain. The Treaty of Paris takes apart most of the Spanish empire. But it leaves one question open: what will happen to the Philippines? The Philippines have about 400 inhabited islands and 9 million people.

The Senate has not yet approved the treaty. Supporters say the Philippines would give American businesses a path into the huge markets of China. McKinley’s top adviser, Mark Hanna, says it would let the United States “take a large slice of the commerce of Asia.” Many missionary groups also want the islands.

Senator Albert Beveridge says Filipinos are not able to govern themselves. Norton calls this a racist justification for taking the islands.

Opponents say that taking the islands would break a basic American idea: that people should govern themselves. Senator George Hoar warns that the treaty would make the United States a “vulgar, commonplace empire.” Opponents also point out that the United States is freeing Cuba but keeping the Philippines.

Filipinos have their own view. Emilio Aguinaldo, a Filipino leader, joined forces with the Americans against Spain. Commodore George Dewey promised that the United States would support Filipino independence. Aguinaldo declared the Philippines independent on June 12, 1898.

What really happened

On February 6, 1899, the Senate approved the treaty by one vote. Norton says that William Jennings Bryan, a Democratic leader, dropped his opposition. He argued that the treaty could open the way to Philippine independence. But President McKinley did not plan to give the Philippines independence.

Filipinos declared independence again and named Aguinaldo president. Fighting between American and Filipino forces began in February 1899. The Philippine-American War lasted three years. About 126,000 U.S. troops fought, and 4,234 American soldiers died. Hundreds of thousands of Filipinos died, most of them civilians. Norton says that both sides used torture and committed what today would be called war crimes.

In 1899, the American Anti-Imperialist League formed to oppose taking the Philippines. Mark Twain and Jane Addams were among the Americans who opposed it. Norton says the imperialists won the debate. In 1902, the Philippines became a colony controlled by the United States. The Philippines became independent in 1946.

Source: Norton Ch. 19, “Consequences of Victory,” Taking the Philippines, Debating the Treaty, and Anti-Imperialist Arguments, pp. 831-839.

Dilemma 5 of 5 · 1904

Debts and Warships: Who Keeps Order in Latin America?

You are there

It is 1904. You are an adviser to President Theodore Roosevelt. Some Latin American nations owe large debts to banks in the United States and Europe. A frequent excuse for foreign intervention is collecting these debts. Both the United States and European countries have interfered in Latin American nations.

Latin Americans respond with the Drago Doctrine of 1902. It is named for Luis María Drago, the foreign minister of Argentina. It says that other countries may not use armed force to collect debts.

Then, in December 1902, German and British warships blockade Venezuela to force it to repay its debts. This defies the Drago Doctrine. It also defies the Monroe Doctrine, a U.S. policy from 1823 that says European nations may not intervene in the Western Hemisphere.

Roosevelt decides that if the United States is going to keep European nations from intervening, “then sooner or later we must keep order [there] ourselves.” Now the Dominican Republic is in a debt crisis.

What really happened

Roosevelt chose to step in. In 1904, he announced the Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine. It said that in certain cases, the United States was justified in intervening in Latin American nations to prevent outsiders from doing so. He said that “chronic wrongdoing” would justify “an international police power.”

Norton says that Roosevelt and other presidents then repeatedly used military force to make sure Latin American nations paid their debts to U.S. and European banks. Norton says Latin Americans were upset by this constant interference.

Source: Norton Ch. 19, “Roosevelt’s ‘Big-Stick’ Diplomacy,” Intervening in Latin America, pp. 845-846.
