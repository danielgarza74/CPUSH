Crowded Cities and New Immigrants

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 1: Understand the effects of urban growth during the Gilded Age, including the problems it created.

Core Objective 2: Describe the “new immigrants” of the late nineteenth century and how they were viewed by American society.

IN MY WORDS: Why did so many people move to cities in the late 1800s, and what problems did they find there?

Reading ACrowds, Dirt, and Disease

After the Civil War, millions of Americans migrated from rural areas to cities. Many had been pushed off the land by new agricultural machinery that reduced the need for farmworkers.

In New York City in 1900, some 2.3 million people—two-thirds of the city’s population—lived in overcrowded tenements, where residents, many of them immigrants, endured poorly ventilated and dimly lit buildings. . . . On average, only one toilet (called a privy) served every twenty people.

Late nineteenth-century cities were filthy and disease-ridden. Garbage and raw sewage were dumped into streets and waterways, causing epidemics of infectious diseases such as cholera, typhoid fever, and yellow fever. In one poor Chicago district at the end of the century, three of every five babies died before their first birthday.

Word help: rural = in the countryside · tenements = crowded, run-down apartment buildings · ventilated = having fresh air · sewage = human waste · epidemics = diseases that spread fast to many people

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 18, “America’s Move to Town” (Crowds, Dirt, and Disease) (p. 780).

Reading BThe New Immigrants

The number of new immigrants soared from just under 3 million annually in the 1870s to more than 9 million per year by 1900. . . . The so-called “old” immigrants, who arrived before 1880, were mainly Protestants and Roman Catholics from northern and western Europe. . . . This pattern changed, however, as the proportion of immigrants from southern and eastern Europe—especially Russia, Poland, Greece, and Italy—rose sharply. After 1890, these “new immigrants” were the majority of newcomers.

Then, as now, many Americans saw the “new immigrants” as a threat to their jobs and way of life. . . . The second, called the Chinese Exclusion Act, barred all Chinese laborers from entering the country for ten years. Thereafter, the exclusion act was periodically renewed.

Word help: proportion = share · majority = more than half · barred = blocked · laborers = workers · periodically renewed = brought back again and again · The second = the second of two immigration laws Congress passed in 1882

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 18, “The New Immigration” (A Surge of Newcomers; The Nativist Response; Chinese Exclusion).

New Ideas and Gilded Age Politics

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 3: Explain how urban growth and the increasingly important role of science influenced leisure activities, cultural life, and social policy in the Gilded Age.

Core Objective 4: Assess how the nature of politics during the Gilded Age contributed to intense political partisanship and political corruption.

IN MY WORDS: What is a political “boss,” and how could a boss lead to corruption?

Reading CFun in the City

The sheer number of city dwellers also helped generate new forms of mass entertainment, especially spectator sports. In the last quarter of the nineteenth century, college football and basketball and professional baseball games began attracting many loyal fans. . . . Still, most urban working-class men spent what little leisure time they had in saloons. By 1900, the United States had more than 325,000 saloons.

Word help: generate = create · spectator sports = sports people watch · urban = in cities · leisure time = free time · saloons = bars

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 18, “Changes in Culture and Thought” (Urban Leisure and Entertainment) (p. 789).

Reading DSocial Darwinism and Its Critics

The influential English social philosopher Herbert Spencer was the first proponent of what came to be called social Darwinism, which celebrated the struggle for existence. . . . The “survival of the fittest,” in Spencer’s memorable phrase, had been the most effective engine of human progress.

What came to be called reform Darwinism found its major advocate in Lester Frank Ward . . . Ward criticized social Darwinism for justifying social inequality, exploitation, and disregard for the well-being of vulnerable individuals and communities. Instead of misusing Darwinism to celebrate ruthless competition, he praised the virtues of cooperation and compassion.

Word help: proponent = supporter · struggle for existence = the fight to survive · advocate = supporter · justifying = making something seem right · vulnerable = easily hurt · compassion = caring for others

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 18, “Changes in Culture and Thought” (Social Darwinism; Reform Darwinism).

Reading EBosses and Party Loyalty

In cities crowded with new immigrant voters, politics were usually controlled by “rings”—small groups of politicians, party leaders, and influential corporate donors who worked together to maintain power . . . Each ring had a powerful party “boss” who used his “machine”—a network of loyal neighborhood activists and officials—to govern local politics. . . . Until his arrest in 1871, Tweed used the Tammany Hall ring to dole out contracts to business allies and jobs to political supporters.

Party loyalty was intense, often extending over generations in many families. A second distinctive element of Gilded Age politics was that both major parties avoided controversial issues or bold initiatives because neither party was dominant. . . . Voter turnout during the Gilded Age was commonly 70 to 80 percent.

Word help: corporate donors = companies that give money · Tweed = William “Boss” Tweed of New York City · dole out = hand out · controversial = causing strong disagreement · dominant = in control · voter turnout = the share of people who vote

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 18, “Gilded Age Politics” (Local Politics and Party Loyalties; Partisan Politics at the National Level).

Reform, Farmers, and the Money Question

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 5: Evaluate the effectiveness of the reforms enacted during this period in promoting “clean” government and limiting the power and political influence of Big Business.

Core Objective 6: Analyze why the money supply became a major political issue during the Gilded Age, especially among small farmers, and describe its impact on politics.

IN MY WORDS: Why did farmers want the government to coin more silver money?

Reading FCleaning Up Government

Hayes announced that it was time “for civil service reform.”

In 1883, the momentum against the spoils system that was generated by Garfield’s assassination enabled George H. Pendleton, a Democratic senator from Ohio, to convince Congress to establish a Civil Service Commission. . . . At least 15 percent of government jobs would now be filled based on competitive tests (the merit system) rather than political favoritism.

The Pendleton Act was a limited first step in cleaning up the patronage process. . . . The Mugwumps sought to reform the patronage system by declaring that all federal jobs would be filled solely on merit.

Word help: Hayes = President Rutherford B. Hayes · civil service = government jobs · spoils system / patronage = giving government jobs to party supporters · merit = skill and ability · Mugwumps = reformers who wanted honest government

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 18, “Corruption and Reform: Hayes to Harrison” (Hayes and Civil Service Reform; The Civil Service Commission; Corruption and a Sex Scandal).

Reading GRegulating the Railroads

Congress followed through, and in 1887, Cleveland signed an act creating the Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC), the first federal agency designed to regulate business activities. . . . One senator, however, called the agency “a delusion and a sham” because its members tended to be former railroad executives. Over time, the ICC came to be ignored, and the railroads continued to charge high rates while making secret pricing deals with large shippers.

Word help: Cleveland = President Grover Cleveland · agency = government office · regulate = make rules for · a delusion and a sham = fake, not real · executives = top bosses · shippers = companies that send goods

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 18, “Corruption and Reform: Hayes to Harrison” (Railroad Regulation) (p. 800).

Reading HFarmers in Trouble

The source of their problems was a prolonged decline in prices earned for their crops, a deflationary trend caused by overproduction and growing international competition in world food markets as well as the inadequate U.S. money supply. . . . Southern cotton plummeted from 31 cents per pound in 1866 to 9 cents in 1886, then to 6 cents in 1893.

In 1873, the Republican-controlled Congress had declared that only gold, not silver, could be used for minting coins. . . . Hard-pressed farmers in the South and Midwest demanded increased coinage of silver, which would inflate the currency supply and thereby raise commodity prices, providing them with more income with which to pay their debts.

Word help: their = farmers’ · prolonged = long · overproduction = growing too much · inadequate = not enough · plummeted = fell fast · minting = making coins · inflate = make bigger · commodity prices = prices for crops

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 18, “Unhappy Farmers and the “Money Question”” (A Vicious Cycle of Depressed Prices and Debt; Silver and Inflation).

Reading IFarmers Organize: From the Grange to the Populists

Kelley helped found the National Grange of the Patrons of Husbandry, better known as the Grange . . . The Granger movement gradually fizzled out as members directed their energies into political action. In the 1880s, Farmers’ Alliances began growing in size and significance.

In 1892, Alliance leaders organized a convention in Omaha, Nebraska, at which they and others formed the People’s Party (Populists). The new party’s platform was truly radical for its time. It called for unlimited coinage of silver, a progressive federal income tax whose rates would rise with income levels, and federal ownership of the railroads and telegraph systems.

Word help: Kelley = Oliver H. Kelley, sent by the Department of Agriculture to visit farmers · fizzled out = slowly ended · radical = calling for big changes · platform = a party’s list of goals · progressive income tax = people with higher incomes pay a higher rate

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 18, “Unhappy Farmers and the “Money Question”” (The Granger Movement; Farmers’ Alliances; The 1892 Election).

Reading JPanic, Bryan, and the Election of 1896

While farmers were channeling their discontent into local and national politics, a major economic crisis occurred in the winter of 1893, when the Philadelphia and Reading Railroad declared bankruptcy, setting off what came to be called the Panic of 1893. It grew into the worst depression the nation had yet experienced.

In his now-famous “Cross of Gold” speech, Bryan claimed to speak for the “producing masses of this nation” against the eastern “financial magnates” who had “enslaved” them by manipulating the money supply. . . . In the end, Bryan won 6.5 million votes—more than any candidate had before—but McKinley won even more: 7.1 million.

Word help: discontent = anger · bankruptcy = being unable to pay debts · depression = a long, very bad economic slump · Bryan = William Jennings Bryan, the candidate of the Democrats and Populists in 1896 · financial magnates = rich bankers · manipulating = secretly controlling

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 18, “Unhappy Farmers and the “Money Question”” (The Panic of 1893; William Jennings Bryan; The Election of 1896).

Why America Wanted an Empire

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 7: Describe the factors that motivated America’s new imperialism after the Civil War.

Core Objective 8: Explain why and how America expanded its influence in the Pacific before the Spanish-American War.

IN MY WORDS: In your own words, what is imperialism?

Reading KThe New Imperialism

In 1902, the British economist J. A. Hobson announced that imperialism had become “the most powerful factor in the current politics of the Western world.” By the 1880s, European nations had conquered most of Africa and Asia.

In 1890, Mahan published The Influence of Sea Power upon History, 1660–1783, a book that greatly influenced U.S. economic and military strategy. . . . He insisted that modern economic development required a powerful navy, global naval bases, foreign commerce, and colonies to provide raw materials and new markets for American products. . . . By 1900, the United States had added eleven new steel battleships

As illustrated by the widespread appeal of Mahan’s views, claims of racial superiority reinforced the imperialist spirit. During the late nineteenth century, many Americans assumed that the Anglo-Saxon or White “race” was superior to others.

Word help: imperialism = a strong nation taking control of other lands · Mahan = Alfred Thayer Mahan, a U.S. Navy captain · commerce = trade · colonies = lands ruled by another country · superiority = being better than others

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 19, “Toward the New Imperialism”.

Reading LTaking Hawaii

In 1875, Hawaiian officials had signed a trade agreement allowing their primary export, sugar, to enter the United States duty-free . . . American sugar planters soon formed an economic elite with growing influence.

Beginning in 1891, Queen Lili‘uokalani, the Hawaiian ruler, tried to restore “Hawaii for the Hawaiians” by restricting the power exercised by U.S. planters in the islands. Two years later, however, Hawaii’s White population (called haoles) revolted and, with the help of Hawaiian allies, overthrew the monarchy when the U.S. ambassador brought in Marines to support the coup.

With McKinley’s blessing, the United States seized control of Hawaii in the summer of 1898, over the protests of Indigenous Hawaiians who resented their nation being annexed without “the consent of the people of the Hawaiian Islands.”

Word help: duty-free = with no tax · economic elite = a small, rich, powerful group · restricting = limiting · monarchy = rule by a king or queen · coup = a sudden takeover of a government · annexed = taken over and added to a country

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 19, “Expansion in the Pacific” (Hawaii).

The Spanish-American War and Its Results

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 9: Identify the causes of the Spanish-American War and its major events.

Core Objective 10: Analyze the consequences of the Spanish-American War for American foreign policy.

IN MY WORDS: Name two events that pushed the United States into war with Spain in 1898.

Reading MThe Road to War

Editors sent their best reporters to Cuba and encouraged them to distort, exaggerate, or even invent stories to attract more readers. Such sensationalist reporting came to be called yellow journalism, and it would play an increasingly important role in shaping U.S. public opinion.

On January 25, the U.S. battleship Maine docked in Havana, the Cuban capital, supposedly on a courtesy call. . . . In the de Lôme letter, the Spanish diplomat called President McKinley “weak and a bidder for the admiration of the crowd . . . On February 15, the battleship Maine exploded without warning and sank in Havana Harbor, with a loss of 266 sailors. Years later, the sinking was ruled an accident caused by an on-board coal explosion, but in 1898 those eager for a war with Spain insisted that the Spanish had destroyed the ship.

The Teller Amendment to the war resolution, named for its author, Senator Henry M. Teller of Colorado, denied any U.S. intention to annex Cuba.

Word help: distort = twist the truth · sensationalist = shocking to get attention · Cuba = an island colony of Spain that was fighting for independence · de Lôme = the Spanish ambassador to the U.S. · diplomat = a person who represents a country · annex = take over

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 19, “The Spanish-American War” (The Cuban Independence Movement; The Political Path to War).

Reading NA Short War

The most flamboyant unit was the First Volunteer Cavalry, better known as the Rough Riders, a special regiment made up of former Ivy League athletes; Irish policemen; ex-convicts; cowboys from Oklahoma and New Mexico; Texas Rangers; gold miners; and Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Pawnee, and Creek Indians. . . . a smaller unit, led by Roosevelt on horseback and including the Rough Riders on foot, seized nearby Kettle Hill.

On December 10, the United States and Spain ended the war by signing the Treaty of Paris. Under its terms, Cuba was to become independent and the United States was to annex Puerto Rico and Guam (a Spanish-controlled island between Hawaii and the Philippines) as new American territories. The United States insisted on governing the Philippines until its future status was decided.

Word help: flamboyant = showy · cavalry = soldiers on horses · regiment = army unit · Roosevelt = Theodore Roosevelt · territories = lands ruled by the U.S. that are not states · status = situation

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 19, “The Spanish-American War” (The Cuban Campaign; Spanish Defeat in Cuba and Puerto Rico).

Reading OWar in the Philippines and the Anti-Imperialists

The grisly American effort to crush Filipino nationalism lasted three years, involved some 126,000 U.S. troops, and took the lives of hundreds of thousands of Filipinos (most of them civilians) and 4,234 American soldiers.

In 1899, several groups combined to form the American Anti-Imperialist League to oppose the annexation of the Philippines and Puerto Rico. Among its 30,000 members were some of the most eminent Americans. . . . Other prominent anti-imperialists included Mark Twain and college presidents Charles Eliot of Harvard and David Starr Jordan of Stanford.

What came to be known as the Open Door policy was outlined in Secretary of State John Hay’s Open Door Note, dispatched in 1899 to his European counterparts. . . . It also appealed to those who opposed imperialism because it pledged to keep China from being carved up by powerful European nations.

Word help: grisly = horrible · Filipino nationalism = Filipinos’ fight for their own nation · civilians = people who are not soldiers · eminent = famous and respected · counterparts = officials in other countries · carved up = divided

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 19, “Consequences of Victory” (The Philippine-American War (1899–1902); Anti-Imperialist Arguments; Imperial Rivalries in East Asia).

Source 1The Philippine Question (Primary Sources)

The first quote is from Senator Albert J. Beveridge of Indiana, speaking to the U.S. Senate in January 1900. He wanted the U.S. to keep the Philippines. The second quote is from Emilio Aguinaldo, the Filipino revolutionary leader, writing to Americans in an American magazine in September 1899.

“The Philippines are ours forever . . . And just beyond the Philippines are China’s illimitable markets. We will not retreat from either. . . . The Philippines give us a base at the door of all the East.”

Roosevelt’s “Big Stick”

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 11: Describe the reasons for Theodore Roosevelt’s rapid rise to the presidency and evaluate the main elements of his foreign policies.

IN MY WORDS: How did Theodore Roosevelt suddenly become president in 1901?

Reading PFrom Vice President to President

The Republicans renominated McKinley and named Roosevelt, now known as “Mr. Imperialism,” their candidate for vice president.

On September 6, 1901, while the president was visiting the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo, New York, a twenty-eight-year-old unemployed anarchist named Leon Czolgosz (pronounced chol-GOSH) approached with a concealed pistol and fired twice at McKinley. . . . On September 14, McKinley died and the combustible Theodore Roosevelt was suddenly the new president.

Word help: renominated = chose again as their candidate · anarchist = a person against all government · concealed = hidden · combustible = easily excited, fiery

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 19, “Roosevelt’s “Big-Stick” Diplomacy” (From Vice President to President).

Reading QThe Canal, the Corollary, and Dollars

As President Roosevelt raged against the “foolish and homicidal corruptionists” in Colombia, the Panamanians revolted against Colombian rule. President Roosevelt aided the Panamanian rebels and signed a treaty with the newly independent Panama to extend the Canal Zone from six to ten miles wide.

In 1904, a crisis over the debts of the Dominican Republic prompted Roosevelt to issue what came to be known as the Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine: the principle, in short, that in certain circumstances the United States was justified in intervening in Latin American nations to prevent outsiders from doing so.

Republican William Howard Taft, who succeeded Roosevelt as president in 1909, continued to promote America’s economic interests abroad, practicing what Roosevelt called “dollar diplomacy.” Taft used the State Department to help American companies and banks invest in foreign countries

Word help: Colombia = the country that ruled Panama before 1903 · Canal Zone = U.S.-controlled land for the Panama Canal · corollary = an added rule · Monroe Doctrine = the 1823 U.S. warning to Europe to stay out of the Americas · intervening = stepping in, often with troops

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 19, “Roosevelt’s “Big-Stick” Diplomacy” (The Panama Canal; Intervening in Latin America; William Howard Taft’s “Dollar Diplomacy”).
