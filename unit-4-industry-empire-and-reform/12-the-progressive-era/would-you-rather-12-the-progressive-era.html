A Voter in 1912: Reform Capitalism or Choose Socialism?

You are there

It is 1912. You are a voter in a big city. Factories and railroads are growing fast, and big businesses have huge power. Reporters have shown how poor workers live and work in terrible conditions.

Almost every candidate says that government must do more. Norton says all four candidates in 1912 shared one idea: modern problems could be fixed only if government stepped in to regulate Big Business and protect people’s welfare. But they do not agree on how far to go.

Some candidates want to keep capitalism but make government regulate it. Norton says progressives wanted to “reform and regulate capitalism, not destroy it.” They feared that if problems were not fixed, angry people would turn to socialism or anarchism.

Eugene V. Debs, the Socialist Party candidate, wants more. He has fought the “monstrous system of capitalism” for years. He wants the government to own the railroads and wants workers to share in the profits. Norton says Debs wants political change, not violent revolution. Many voters think the Socialist Party is the only real choice, because the two big parties have few differences.

Other Americans are afraid of socialism. They connect it with the radicalism and revolutions of Europe.

What really happened

Woodrow Wilson won the election. He got 435 electoral votes. Theodore Roosevelt got 88 and William Howard Taft got 8. Debs, running for the fourth time, won over 900,000 votes. That was more than twice as many as four years earlier. But he did not win a single vote in the Electoral College.

Socialists did well in local elections. In 1912, about 1,150 Socialist Party candidates won local and state offices, including eighteen mayors. The party had 118,000 members who paid dues, twice as many as the year before.

Norton says the progressives changed the nation too. They set up the idea that local, state, and federal governments should protect Americans from abuse by powerful businesses and corrupt politicians.

Source: Norton Ch. 20, “Woodrow Wilson: A Progressive Southerner,” The 1912 Election and Eugene V. Debs, pp. 893-894.

Hull House in 1889: Help From Outside or Move Next Door?

You are there

It is 1889. You are Jane Addams, a college-educated woman from a well-off family in Chicago. You want to help poor people, especially immigrants who have just arrived from Europe.

Many people already help the poor from the outside. Churches and charities give out aid. Norton says Salvation Army centers run soup kitchens to feed the poor and day nurseries for the children of working mothers. Many Christians believe they have a duty to help the poorest and weakest people.

Another idea has come from Great Britain. It is called a “settlement house.” Volunteers live in a community center in the neighborhood they want to help.

You and your friend Ellen Gates Starr could turn a run-down two-story mansion in a shabby Chicago neighborhood into a settlement house. You feel an “impulse to share the lives of the poor.”

What really happened

In 1889, Addams and Starr opened Hull House. About 50,000 poor people came through its doors in the first year. Hull House had a nursery for babies of working mothers, health clinics, lectures, music lessons, art studios, an employment bureau, job training, a gym, a coffeehouse, a savings bank, and a public bath. Everyone who lived there was treated as an equal. By the early 1900s, there were hundreds of settlement houses in American cities.

Addams reported that charities and churches were “totally inadequate to deal with the vast numbers of the city’s disinherited.” She and other settlement house leaders soon added political reform to their work. They lobbied for city parks and playgrounds, neighborhood clean-up days, and laws to improve living conditions. By 1940, 320,000 people a year used Hull House. Addams became the first American woman to receive the Nobel Peace Prize.

Source: Norton Ch. 20, “The Varied Sources of Progressivism,” Religious Activism and Social Responsibility and Settlement Houses, pp. 867-868.

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1900

Galveston After the Storm: Elected Leaders or a Commission?

You are there

It is 1900. You are a leader in Galveston, Texas. A hurricane and tidal wave have just hit your city. More than 8,000 people have died. Norton says it is the largest natural disaster in American history.

Norton says the city government had not made the changes that were recommended to protect the city from hurricanes.

Reformers have a new idea. It is called the commission system. A small board of commissioners would run the city departments, such as sanitation, police, and utilities. The commissioners would have both the power to make rules and the power to carry them out. Supporters want government to run more like a business, with trained experts.

Others want to keep elected officials who speak for each neighborhood. They worry about giving that up. For many working-class voters, party politics is their main way to take part in city government.

What really happened

In 1901, Galveston became the first city to adopt the commission system. By 1911, more than sixty cities had done the same. An even more popular idea was the city-manager plan. In it, an appointed administrator runs the city under policies set by an elected council and mayor. Staunton, Virginia, adopted the first one in 1908. After a flood, Dayton, Ohio, became the first large city to adopt it in 1913.

Norton says these changes had a downside. They moved control from elected officials who spoke for neighborhoods to commissioners and experts. That separated city government from party politics, which had been the main way many working-class voters took part. Commissioners and managers also focused on cutting costs more than adding services, even when more services were needed.

Source: Norton Ch. 20, “Progressives’ Aims and Achievements,” The Efficiency Movement in Business and Government and Municipal Reform, pp. 874-875.

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1912

Republican Progressives in 1912: Stay With Taft or Follow Roosevelt?

You are there

It is June 1912. You are a progressive Republican at the national convention in Chicago. Four years ago, President Theodore Roosevelt asked your party to nominate his friend William Howard Taft. Taft promised to continue Roosevelt’s policies.

Now Roosevelt says Taft has not “carried out my work.” He is angry that Taft filled the cabinet with corporate lawyers and fired Gifford Pinchot, the head of the forest service. Roosevelt wants stronger federal control of big corporations. Voters in a dozen or so states picked their choice in primaries, and Roosevelt won all but two of them.

Taft’s supporters say Roosevelt is unfair. Norton says Taft is a “conservative progressive.” His government saved more federal land in four years than Roosevelt’s did in nearly eight. It filed almost twice as many anti-trust suits. It set up a Children’s Bureau and supported women’s suffrage and the right of workers to join unions.

Taft’s side wins in the thirty-six states where party bosses run the conventions. Taft wins the nomination easily. Roosevelt calls his opponents thieves and storms out with his delegates. He is ready to start a new party.

What really happened

About 150 Roosevelt delegates met at Orchestra Hall. Hundreds more joined them. They formed the Progressive Party. Roosevelt said he felt “fit as a bull moose,” so reporters called it the Bull Moose Party. Its platform asked for a minimum “living wage” for hourly workers, women’s suffrage, campaign finance reform, and insurance for sickness, unemployment, and disability.

In the fall, Wilson won with 435 electoral votes. Roosevelt got 88 and Taft got 8. Norton says the split weakened the progressive side of the Republican Party. The party that won the White House back in 1920 was more conservative.

The new party also faced a fight over race. In the South, it kept African Americans out of its delegations. Jane Addams said a party for human rights should not look like it did not stand for Black Americans’ rights.

Source: Norton Ch. 20, “Progressivism under Roosevelt and Taft,” The Transition from Roosevelt to Taft through The Progressive Party, pp. 888-891.

Women’s Suffrage in 1913: Wait for the States or Push for an Amendment?

You are there

It is 1913. You are a leader in the women’s suffrage movement. Women still cannot vote in most of the country. Susan B. Anthony and Elizabeth Cady Stanton formed the National Woman Suffrage Association in 1869 to win an amendment to the U.S. Constitution.

So far, women have won the vote one state at a time, mostly in the West. Wyoming gave women full voting rights in 1890. By 1912, nine western states had done it. No state east of the Mississippi River has given women the vote in all elections.

Woodrow Wilson is about to become president. He says that voting rights for women should be left to the states. He does not want a constitutional amendment, even though two of his daughters are suffragists.

Alice Paul, a Quaker social worker with a doctoral degree, wants to push harder. She wants suffragists to protest in public, target politicians who do not support suffrage, and be willing to be arrested.

What really happened

Alice Paul pushed hard. In 1913, she organized thousands of suffragists to march in Washington, D.C., to protest Wilson’s inauguration. Spectators taunted, spat on, and roughed up the marchers, and a cavalry unit was sent to restore order. Not all marchers were treated equally. Some leaders placed African American women at the back, because they did not want to upset southern voters. Ida B. Wells did not accept that. She joined the front of the Illinois group.

State campaigns kept winning too. In 1917, New York became the first state east of the Mississippi River to give women the vote in all elections. That year, Paul and her followers began carrying signs in front of the White House, five days a week, for six months. Then Wilson ordered their arrest. About sixty suffragists were jailed. Paul was sentenced to seven months. She went on a hunger strike, and prison officials force-fed her through a tube. After a flood of news stories and public criticism, Wilson pardoned her and the others.

Source: Norton Ch. 20, “Woodrow Wilson: A Progressive Southerner,” The Vote for Women, pp. 898-899.
