Who Were the Progressives?

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 1: Assess the motives of progressive reformers and how they reflected the underlying values of the progressive movement.

Core Objective 2: Explain the sources of thought and social activism that contributed to the progressive movement.

IN MY WORDS: In your own words, what did progressives want the government to do?

Reading AThe Progressive Impulse

Progressives—many of whom came from middle-class backgrounds—were liberals, not revolutionaries. They feared that if social ills were not addressed, social unrest would lead people to embrace revolutionary ideas such as socialism or anarchism . . . They wanted to reform and regulate capitalism, not destroy it. Their motivations were often religious.

What progressives shared was the assumption that governments must become more active in addressing the problems created by rapid urban and industrial growth. Chicago’s Jane Addams, a leading progressive reformer, reported that charities and churches were “totally inadequate to deal with the vast numbers of the city’s disinherited.”

Word help: social ills = problems in society · unrest = anger and protest · socialism = government or workers owning businesses · regulate = make rules for · capitalism = private ownership of business for profit · inadequate = not enough · disinherited = poor people left out

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “The Progressive Impulse”.

Reading BThe Muckrakers

The so-called muckrakers were America’s first investigative journalists, and they were at the heart of progressivism. Their aggressive reporting educated readers about political and corporate wrongdoing and revealed “how the other half lives”—a description drawn from the title of an influential exposé of the terrible living conditions experienced by immigrants in New York City, written by photojournalist Jacob Riis, a Danish immigrant.

McClure’s and other muckraking magazines and newspapers investigated corporate monopolies and crooked political machines while exposing the miserable conditions in which the working poor lived and labored.

Word help: investigative journalists = reporters who dig for hidden facts · corporate = of big companies · exposé = a report that reveals wrongdoing · crooked = dishonest · political machines = groups that control city politics

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “The Varied Sources of Progressivism” (Muckraking Journalism).

Picture 1“Fire on Hell Street” by Jacob Riis

Norton’s caption: “This photograph by Jacob Riis revealed the cramped and unsanitary living conditions faced by many immigrants living in tenements on New York City’s lower East Side.” (It is a primary source photograph.)

What to look for: Look at the room, the furniture, and the people’s clothes · cramped = crowded with little space · unsanitary = dirty and unhealthy · tenements = crowded apartment buildings

Norton, Ch. 20, “The Varied Sources of Progressivism,” photograph “Fire on Hell Street” by Jacob Riis. Primary source.

Reading CFaith and Settlement Houses

Still another element of progressivism was religious activism directed at achieving social justice—the idea that Christians had an ethical obligation to help the poorest and most vulnerable members of society. A related ideal was the social gospel, the belief that religious institutions and individual Christians should treat all people like brothers and sisters in Christ.

Of these, Hull House was America’s most famous. . . . Two college-educated women from privileged backgrounds, Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr, founded Hull House to address the needs of the working poor, especially newly arrived European immigrants.

Word help: social justice = fairness for everyone in society · ethical obligation = a duty to do what is right · vulnerable = easily hurt · Of these = of the settlement houses, community centers that helped the poor · privileged = rich and comfortable

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “The Varied Sources of Progressivism” (Religious Activism and Social Responsibility; Settlement Houses).

Reading DWomen Demand the Vote

As more middle-class and elite women graduated from college and—through their churches, synagogues, and women’s organizations—became involved in progressive causes, they gave the suffrage movement new momentum.

Susan B. Anthony and Elizabeth Cady Stanton had founded the National Woman Suffrage Association (NWSA) in 1869 to promote a women’s suffrage amendment to the Constitution. The NWSA agitated for more than the right to vote, however. Its members also campaigned for higher pay for working women and for laws helping abused wives secure divorces.

Word help: elite = rich and powerful · suffrage = the right to vote · momentum = energy that keeps something moving · amendment = a change to the Constitution · agitated = pushed hard

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “The Varied Sources of Progressivism” (The Women’s Suffrage Movement).

What the Progressives Achieved

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 3: Identify the reforms the progressives achieved at the local, state, and national levels, and how those reforms advanced the larger cause of social justice.

IN MY WORDS: Name one reform that gave ordinary voters more power.

Reading EGiving Power to Voters

One proposal was the direct primary, which would allow all members of a party to vote on the party’s nominees, rather than the traditional practice in which an inner circle of party leaders chose the candidates, often behind closed doors.

Citizens could sign petitions to have a proposal put on the ballot (the initiative) and then vote it up or down (the referendum). Another progressive innovation was the recall, whereby corrupt or incompetent elected officials could be removed by a public petition and vote.

Under the Constitution, state legislatures elected senators, a process frequently corrupted by lobbyists and vote buying. In 1913, thanks to the efforts of progressives, the Seventeenth Amendment, providing for the direct election of senators by voters, was ratified and became law.

Word help: nominees = candidates chosen by a party · petitions = papers many people sign to ask for something · incompetent = not able to do the job · lobbyists = people paid to influence lawmakers · ratified = approved

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “Progressives’ Aims and Achievements” (Political Reforms) (p. 871).

Reading FEfficiency and the Income Tax

A second major theme of progressivism was the “gospel of efficiency.” Its champion was Frederick Winslow Taylor . . . The goal of what came to be called Taylorism was to improve productivity and profits, raise pay for the most efficient workers, and reduce the likelihood of worker strikes.

They advocated the creation of a “progressive” federal income tax—so called not because of the idea’s association with the progressive movement, but because the tax rates are based on a sliding scale; that is, the rates “progress” or rise as income levels rise, thus forcing the rich to pay more. . . . It became law in 1913 with state ratification of the Sixteenth Amendment.

Word help: efficiency = getting the most work done with the least waste · productivity = how much gets made · advocated = argued for · sliding scale = rates that change with income · They = progressives

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “Progressives’ Aims and Achievements” (The Efficiency Movement in Business and Government; The “Progressive” Income Tax).

Reading GThe Triangle Fire and the NAACP

On March 25, 1911, a raging fire broke out at the ten-story Asch Building in New York City. . . . Most of the workers were teenage women and almost all were Jewish, Italian, or Russian immigrants. . . . escape routes were limited because the owners kept one of the two stairway doors locked to prevent theft. After thirty minutes of sheer horror, 146 garment workers died. . . . Their efforts resulted in dozens of new city and state regulations dealing with fire hazards, working conditions, women workers’ rights, and child labor.

In February 1909, Ida B. Wells, Mary Church Terrell, W.E.B. Du Bois, and other Black leaders joined White progressives such as the social worker Mary White Ovington, Florence Kelly, and the labor reformer William English Walling to establish the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP).

Word help: the workers = the 500 workers at the Triangle Shirtwaist clothing factory in that building · garment = clothing · Their efforts = the work of reformers like Frances Perkins after the fire · regulations = rules · hazards = dangers

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “Progressives’ Aims and Achievements” (The Triangle Shirtwaist Factory Fire; Racial Violence and the Origins of Modern Civil Rights Organizing).

Source 1Ida Tarbell on Standard Oil, 1904 (Primary Source)

Ida Tarbell was a muckraker. In 1904 she published The History of the Standard Oil Company, a famous book about John D. Rockefeller’s oil trust. This is from its conclusion.

“Ever since 1872 the organisation has appeared in politics only to oppose legislation obviously for the public good. . . . There is something alarming . . . in knowing that so large a body of young men in this country are consciously or unconsciously growing up with the idea that business is war and that morals have nothing to do with its practice.”

Word help: the organisation = Standard Oil · legislation = laws · public good = what helps most people · alarming = scary · morals = ideas of right and wrong

Ida Tarbell, The History of the Standard Oil Company, 1904. In Norton, Ch. 20, “Progressives’ Aims and Achievements,” Comparing Perspectives: “Muckrakers and Reformers” (p. 872).

Roosevelt and Taft

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 4: Describe how Presidents Theodore Roosevelt and William Howard Taft differed in their approaches to progressive reform.

IN MY WORDS: What were the “Three Cs” of Roosevelt’s Square Deal?

Reading HRoosevelt’s Square Deal

Theodore Roosevelt was the first president to use executive power to rein in Big Business. . . . He endorsed a Square Deal for “every man, great or small, rich or poor.”

Roosevelt’s Square Deal program featured what were called the “Three Cs”: greater government control of corporations, enhanced conservation of natural resources, and new regulations to protect consumers against contaminated food and medicines.

In his view, some big businesses were bad not because they were big but because their executives acted unethically or unfairly. . . . He created fifty federal wildlife refuges, approved five new national parks and fifty-one federal bird sanctuaries, and designated eighteen national monuments, including the Grand Canyon.

Word help: executive power = the power of the president · rein in = control · endorsed = supported · conservation = protecting nature · contaminated = dirty or poisoned · unethically = in a wrong or dishonest way · refuges = safe places for animals

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “Progressivism under Roosevelt and Taft” (Taming Big Business; Curbing the Trusts; Environmental Conservation).

Reading ITaft: A Different Style

Taft was a conservative progressive who vowed to preserve capitalism by protecting “the right of property” and the “right of liberty.” In practice, this meant that he was even more determined than Roosevelt to support “the spirit of commercial freedom” against monopolistic trusts, but he was not interested in pushing for additional reforms.

But he proved less skillful than Roosevelt in dealing with legislators and lobbyists. Taft confessed that he did not know how to use the “bully pulpit” that Roosevelt had perfected. . . . Taft’s administration actually preserved more federal land in four years than Roosevelt’s had in nearly eight; and it filed almost twice as many anti-trust suits

Word help: conservative = careful, wanting less change · vowed = promised · monopolistic trusts = giant companies that control an industry · bully pulpit = using the presidency to rally the public · anti-trust suits = court cases to break up trusts

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “Progressivism under Roosevelt and Taft” (A Life of Public Service; Taft and the Tariff; The Taft-Roosevelt Feud).

Reading JThe Bull Moose Party

At Roosevelt’s urging, the pro-Roosevelt crusaders launched a new political party that would promote the most advanced ideas of the era. He assured them that he felt “fit as a bull moose,” leading journalists to nickname the new Progressive Party the “Bull Moose Party.”

The Progressive Party supported a progressive platform: a minimum “living wage” for hourly workers; women’s suffrage; campaign finance reform; and a federal system of social insurance to protect people against sickness, unemployment, and the effects of disabilities.

Word help: crusaders = people fighting for a cause · platform = a party’s list of goals · living wage = pay high enough to live on · social insurance = government help when people are sick or out of work

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “Progressivism under Roosevelt and Taft” (The Progressive Party).

Wilson’s New Freedom

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 5: Explain how President Woodrow Wilson’s progressive policies affected the role of government—and the presidency in particular—in the Progressive Era.

IN MY WORDS: How was Wilson’s New Freedom different from Roosevelt’s view of big business?

Reading KThe Election of 1912

The contest then settled into a debate over Theodore Roosevelt’s New Nationalism and Woodrow Wilson’s New Freedom. Wilson favored small government and states’ rights, arguing that federal intervention in society should be a last resort. His program aimed to restore economic competition by eliminating all trusts rather than just those that misbehaved. . . . Where Roosevelt believed that bigness in business was inevitable, Wilson insisted that huge, “heartless” industries needed to be broken up.

Word help: federal intervention = the national government stepping in · last resort = the final choice when nothing else works · eliminating = getting rid of · inevitable = sure to happen

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “Woodrow Wilson: A Progressive Southerner” (The 1912 Election).

Reading LWilson’s Reform Laws

His efforts resulted in the Underwood-Simmons Tariff Act (1913), which lowered tariff rates on almost 1,000 imported products. . . . After much dickering, Congress passed the Federal Reserve Act on December 23, 1913. It created a national banking system with twelve regional districts, each of which had its own Federal Reserve Bank owned by member banks in the district.

Wilson decided to make a strong Federal Trade Commission (FTC) the cornerstone of his anti-trust program. Created in 1914, the five-member FTC defined “unfair trade practices” and issued “cease-and-desist” orders to businesses when it found evidence of such practices. . . . The Clayton Anti-Trust Act declared that labor unions were not to be viewed as “monopolies in restraint of trade,” as courts had treated them since 1890.

Word help: His = Wilson’s · tariff = tax on imported goods · dickering = arguing over details · cornerstone = most important part · cease-and-desist = an order to stop · in restraint of trade = blocking fair business

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “Woodrow Wilson: A Progressive Southerner” (Wilson’s Economic Policies; Anti-Trust Actions).

Reading MThe Limits of Wilson’s Progressivism

Like many other progressives, Wilson showed little interest in addressing the daily discrimination and violence that African Americans and Mexican Americans faced. . . . He was the first president since the Civil War who openly endorsed discrimination against African Americans.

Activists for women’s suffrage were also disappointed in President Wilson. Despite having two daughters who were suffragists, he insisted that the issue of voting rights for women should be left to the states rather than embodied in a constitutional amendment.

Word help: discrimination = unfair treatment because of race or other differences · endorsed = supported · suffragists = people working for women’s right to vote · embodied in = written into

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 20, “Woodrow Wilson: A Progressive Southerner” (Progressivism for Whites Only; The Vote for Women).
