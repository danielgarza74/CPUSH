Dilemma 1 of 5 · 1889

Congress and the Trusts: Hands Off or Step In?

You are there

It is 1889. You are a member of Congress. American businesses are growing very fast. John D. Rockefeller’s Standard Oil Company already controls more than 90 percent of the nation’s oil refining. It is a monopoly, a business so large that it controls an entire industry.

In 1882, Rockefeller organized the Standard Oil Trust. A trust lets one group of leaders manage more than thirty companies at the same time. Today Americans call any huge corporation a trust, and trusts are drawing intense criticism.

Some business leaders say that big combinations are good. Rockefeller believed his competitors were inefficient. Financier J. Pierpont Morgan said that “bitter, destructive competition” always led to “destruction and ruin.” So far, the government has mostly stayed out of business. Norton calls this a laissez-faire (hands-off) period.

Others say that competition is the driving engine of capitalism. It forces businesses to make better products at the lowest cost. When one company controls a whole industry, there is little competition left.

Should Congress step in? What would you choose?

What really happened

Congress chose to act. In 1890, it passed the Sherman Anti-Trust Act. The law said that efforts to monopolize an industry and “restrain” competition were illegal. But the law’s wording was so vague that Norton says its rules were “toothless.”

Big business kept growing. In 1899, Rockefeller declared, “It is too late to argue about the advantages of [huge] industrial combinations. They are a necessity.” That year he put his business empire under Standard Oil of New Jersey, a gigantic holding company. By 1904, his companies controlled almost 85 percent of the petroleum industry.

Source: Norton Ch. 16, “The Rise of Big Business,” Fighting Competition and John D. Rockefeller, pp. 697-699.

Dilemma 2 of 5 · 1886

Workers in 1886: One Big Union or a Craft Union?

You are there

It is 1886. You are a wage worker. Many workers are asking for shorter hours and better pay. You want a union to speak for you, and you have two main choices.

The Knights of Labor, led by Terence Powderly, welcomes almost all workers, skilled and unskilled. It leaves out only lawyers, doctors, bankers, liquor sellers, and Chinese workers. In the early 1880s it grew from about 100,000 members to more than 700,000. Powderly says that higher wages are not enough: “this matter will not be settled until the wage system is abolished.” He wants workers and bosses to settle disputes by peaceful talks and arbitration, not by strikes.

The American Federation of Labor (AFL) was just started by delegates from twenty-five craft unions, which are unions of skilled workers. Each union is mostly free to act on its own. Its president, Samuel Gompers, focuses on concrete gains: higher wages, shorter hours, and better working conditions. Craft union leaders worry that joining with unskilled workers would mean losing their identity and bargaining power.

Which would you join? What would you choose?

What really happened

The Knights of Labor grew to be the nation’s largest union, but business leaders fought it. Norton says they used their influence to discredit Powderly’s leadership, and Knights membership plummeted. Most other labor leaders believed that unions could win only by confronting management with demonstrations and strikes.

The AFL grew slowly at first. By 1900 it claimed 500,000 members, and in 1920 it reached 4 million. Even then, it included less than 15 percent of the nation’s non-farm workers. Industries such as steel, textiles, tobacco, and meatpacking stayed almost untouched by unions. The Knights did leave one lasting idea: the industrial union, which includes all skilled and unskilled workers in an industry. Gompers never opposed industrial unions, and the United Mine Workers became an important AFL member.

Source: Norton Ch. 16, “Organized Labor,” The Knights of Labor and Samuel Gompers and the AFL, pp. 711-715.

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1892

Steelworker at Homestead: Fight the Terms or Accept Them?

You are there

It is the summer of 1892. You are a skilled worker at Andrew Carnegie’s steel mill in Homestead, Pennsylvania, near Pittsburgh. Your union, the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers, is the nation’s largest craft union. It had friendly relations with the company until Henry Clay Frick became chief executive in 1889. Frick is proud to be the most anti-labor executive in the nation.

Now your union contract is up for renewal. The company wants to cut costs by using machines, so it needs fewer highly paid skilled workers, even though it is making high profits. It also wants to cut wages. Even the mill manager, William Jones, opposes cutting wages. Carnegie, who once said he sympathized with unions, now wants to end the union so he will not have to negotiate. Frick has a twelve-foot fence topped with barbed wire built around the plant.

People see this in two ways. Terence Powderly of the Knights of Labor says critics should put themselves in the workers’ place. The work takes great skill, and a few years at the furnace can cost a man his eyesight. He calls the fence a “prison-pen.” A Philadelphia newspaper says a strike would be unwise. It would be “the weak against the strong,” it says, and four-fifths of the nation’s workers earn even less than the Homestead men.

What really happened

The talks failed, and a strike began. Frick hired 316 Pinkerton agents, from a private detective agency, to guard the mill. Before dawn on July 6, 1892, the Pinkertons floated up the river on two barges. Thousands of union members and supporters waited on shore. A fourteen-hour gun battle killed seven workers and four Pinkertons, and dozens were wounded. The Pinkertons surrendered.

A week later, Pennsylvania’s governor sent 8,500 National Guard troops to the mill. Frick said, “I will never recognize the union, never, never!” Then an anarchist named Alexander Berkman tried to kill Frick, and much of the public sympathy for the strikers faded. The workers ended the strike on November 20 and accepted the company’s harsh wage cuts. Only a fifth of the strikers got their jobs back. After Homestead, none of Carnegie’s steel plants had a union.

Source: Norton Ch. 16, “Organized Labor,” The Homestead Steel Strike, pp. 715-717.

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1879

After Reconstruction: Leave the South or Stay?

You are there

It is 1879. You are an African American living in the South. Federal Reconstruction has collapsed, and White supremacy is returning. Many Black southerners are poor farmers who work land they do not own.

Benjamin “Pap” Singleton was born into slavery in Tennessee in 1809. He escaped, and after the Civil War he came back to Tennessee. He decided that African Americans could never gain equal treatment if they stayed in the former Confederacy. He learned that land in Kansas was selling for $1.25 an acre, and in 1878 he led 200 colonists there. Now thousands of people are heading west. They are being called “Exodusters,” because they are making an exodus from the South.

But going is a big risk. One traveler called the Great Plains “a treeless desert,” scorching in the day and “chill and piercing” cold at night. The trip is long, and you would leave the region you know. Many others decide to stay where they are.

What really happened

About 6,000 African Americans arrived in Kansas in 1879, and as many as 20,000 followed the next year. By the early 1880s the movement had faded. Many settlers were unprepared for the harsh plains. Their homesteads were often too small to support a family, so most Black farmers also had to work as laborers for White ranchers. Drought, grasshoppers, prairie fires, and dust storms caused crop failures. So many newcomers arrived that there were not enough houses, stores, or water. Many gave up their land and moved to the few cities in Kansas.

Kansas was not the “promised land” that people had been led to expect, but Norton says it was better than what they had known in the South. As one Exoduster minister said, “We had rather suffer and be free.” Most African Americans, however, stayed in the South. Norton says that for them, survival meant wearing a “mask of deference” under White supremacy.

Source: Norton Ch. 17, “The Settling of the New West,” The African American Migration, pp. 747-748.

Dilemma 5 of 5 · Late 1890s

Answering Jim Crow: Washington or Du Bois?

You are there

It is the late 1890s. You are an African American living in the South. White southerners are taking away the rights of Black citizens. Mississippi’s 1890 plan used a residence requirement, a poll tax, and reading tests judged by White registrars to keep Black people from voting. Other states copied it. In 1896, the Supreme Court ruled in Plessy v. Ferguson that states could pass laws to separate the races. “Whites Only” and “Colored Only” signs are going up.

Deadly violence goes with these laws. From 1890 to 1899, the United States averaged 188 lynchings per year. In 1898 in Wilmington, North Carolina, a White mob overthrew the elected city government and killed sixty people in Black neighborhoods.

Two Black leaders disagree about how to respond. Booker T. Washington, president of Tuskegee Institute, spoke in Atlanta in 1895. He told African Americans to “cast down your [water] bucket where you are,” and to build skills in farming, trades, business, household service, and the professions. He said that fighting for “social equality” would be “the extremest folly.” African Americans should first become self-sufficient. Civil rights would have to wait.

W.E.B. Du Bois, a professor at Atlanta University, disagrees. He says leaders should use “ceaseless agitation” to win the right to vote and civil equality. Education should not be only job training. It should develop bold leaders who will challenge segregation and discrimination. He demands that disenfranchisement and legal segregation end immediately.

What really happened

Washington answered his critics. He said that Du Bois, a New Englander, never understood how brutal southern racism could be. A more militant strategy in the South, he said, would only get more Black people lynched. Du Bois and other critics also did not know that Washington secretly paid for legal efforts against Jim Crow laws. He said these “quiet efforts” were more successful and realistic than the “brass band” approach of Du Bois.

The argument did not end. Norton says it showed tensions that would divide the civil rights movement of the 1900s: militancy versus conciliation, separatism versus assimilation, and social justice versus economic opportunity. By 1915, when Washington died, leadership of the Black community was passing to Du Bois and others who demanded true equality. Other African Americans worked for change in their own ways. Ida B. Wells, a journalist, began a crusade against lynching.

Source: Norton Ch. 17, “The Origins of Jim Crow Segregation,” Booker T. Washington and W.E.B. Du Bois, pp. 742-744.
