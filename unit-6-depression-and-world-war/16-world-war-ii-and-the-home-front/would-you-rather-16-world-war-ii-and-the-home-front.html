Dilemma 1 of 5 · 1941

January 1941: Send Military Aid to Britain or Stay Out?

You are there

It is January 1941. You are a member of Congress. Dictators now rule Germany and Italy. In Japan, military leaders who want to conquer Asia are in charge. By 1941, only a dozen or so democratic nations are left in the world.

In 1940, Germany took over most of western Europe, including France. Great Britain now stands alone against Hitler. Britain is also running low on cash to pay for weapons.

Most Americans want to stay out of another war. Isolationists formed the America First Committee in 1940. It has about 800,000 members.

President Roosevelt asks Congress to pass the Lend-Lease Act. It would let the president lend or lease military equipment to any country whose defense he thinks is vital to the defense of the United States. He says the United States would help Britain, and China in its war against Japan, while keeping America out of the war. He calls the United States “the great arsenal of democracy.”

Some Americans fear this plan would pull the United States into the wars. Members of one isolationist group, the Mother’s Crusade, kneel in prayer in front of the Capitol. They are asking Congress to defeat the bill.

What really happened

In early March 1941, Congress approved the Lend-Lease Act. The New York Times said the United States had ended its “great retreat” from world leadership. The newspaper said isolationism had failed.

From 1941 to 1945, Lend-Lease shipped $50 billion in supplies to Great Britain, the Soviet Union, France, China, and other Allied nations. In June 1941, Germany invaded the Soviet Union. Roosevelt insisted on including the Soviets in Lend-Lease.

On December 7, 1941, Japan attacked Pearl Harbor. Norton says the attack destroyed the isolationist movement. The United States declared war on Japan. Three days later, Germany and Italy declared war on the United States. The separate wars in Asia, Europe, and North Africa became one global conflict.

Source: Norton Ch. 24, “From Isolationism to Intervention,” The Lend-Lease Act (1941), pp. 1060-1061, and America Declares War, p. 1068.

1941: March on Washington or Accept a Deal?

You are there

It is 1941. You are A. Philip Randolph. You started the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, the largest African American labor union. You are its leader.

Defense factories are hiring workers. But only 3 percent of defense workers are African Americans. You have threatened to bring 100,000 people to march on Washington. The march would demand an end to racial discrimination in defense industries.

The Roosevelt administration wants to stop the march. It offers you a bargain. If you call off the march, President Roosevelt will issue an executive order. An executive order is a command from the president. This one would require equal treatment in the hiring of workers.

What really happened

Randolph called off the march. Roosevelt issued the executive order. Within a year, African Americans were working at high-paying jobs in aircraft factories and arms plants.

But Norton says the war did not end racism in the United States. African Americans in industry were forced to take the dirtiest and most dangerous jobs. They also met constant discrimination when they looked for housing.

The armed forces were still segregated in 1941. About a million African Americans served during the war, but most were given menial duties. After the war, the excellent record of the Tuskegee Airmen helped push leaders to desegregate the armed forces.

Source: Norton Ch. 24, “Mobilization at Home,” African Americans during the War, pp. 1071-1072, and Racial Tensions in the Military, pp. 1072-1073.

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1942

1942: Invade France Now or Invade North Africa First?

You are there

It is 1942. You are President Franklin Roosevelt. The United States is now in the war. Germany controls much of Europe. German submarines are sinking many ships that cross the Atlantic to Britain. Germany is also trying to conquer the Soviet Union, but Soviet resistance has slowed it down.

The Soviet people are carrying most of the fighting. Their leader, Joseph Stalin, wants the Americans and British to open a “second front.” That means attacking the Germans in western Europe. It would force Hitler to pull soldiers, tanks, and warplanes away from the Soviet Union.

American planners want to attack France before the end of 1942. The British worry about moving too fast against battle-hardened German troops. Prime Minister Winston Churchill warns that losing a fight on the French coast could be the only way to lose the war. He proposes a joint American and British invasion of North Africa instead. German and Italian armies there are not nearly as strong as those in Europe.

What really happened

Roosevelt chose B. Norton says he was persuaded by Churchill’s plan. In early November 1942, 100,000 American and British troops landed in Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia. On May 12, 1943, some 250,000 Germans and Italians surrendered, and North Africa was in Allied control.

Norton says the decision to delay the second front caused conflict among the Allies. Stalin kept urging the Allies to invade western Europe.

The invasion of France came later. On June 6, 1944, known as D-day, some 5,300 Allied ships carrying 370,000 soldiers and sailors reached the coast of Normandy. Norton calls the Normandy invasion a turning point in the war.

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1943

1943: Take Every Island or Leapfrog Past Some?

You are there

It is 1943. You are a U.S. military planner in the Pacific. You work for General Douglas MacArthur and Admiral Nimitz. The war in the Pacific is fought across a huge ocean full of islands.

In 1942, Japan’s armies swept across the Pacific and Southeast Asia. In June 1942, the Americans won the Battle of Midway. It was the first major defeat for the Japanese navy in 350 years.

On August 7, 1942, some 19,000 U.S. marines landed on Guadalcanal Island. The Japanese had an air base there. The fighting was savage and lasted until February 1943. It was the first defeat for the Japanese army. About 20,000 Japanese and 1,752 Americans were lost.

Japan holds many more islands between you and Japan. The brutal fighting on Guadalcanal showed how hard the Japanese would fight to keep each one.

What really happened

MacArthur and Nimitz chose B. They called it “leapfrogging” or “island-hopping.” They took the most important islands and bypassed the others. Nimitz said the isolated Japanese bases would “wither on the vine.”

On June 15, 1944, U.S. forces took Tinian, Guam, and Saipan in the Mariana Islands. Saipan was the site of one of the deadliest battles in the Pacific. American B-29 bombers could now take off from there and strike Japan itself.

The fighting stayed brutal as U.S. forces got closer to Japan. Iwo Jima took almost six weeks and cost nearly 7,000 American lives. Okinawa took almost three months. More than 150,000 Japanese were killed, and about 49,000 Americans were killed, wounded, or missing.

Source: Norton Ch. 24, “The Pacific War,” General MacArthur’s Pacific Strategy, p. 1090, and The Battles of Iwo Jima and Okinawa, p. 1092.

February 1945: Press Stalin on Poland or Accept His Terms?

You are there

It is February 1945. You are President Franklin Roosevelt. You are at the Yalta Conference in Crimea with Winston Churchill and Joseph Stalin. Allied armies are closing in on Berlin.

The three leaders have agreed that when Germany surrenders, the Soviets will occupy eastern Germany. The Americans, British, and French will control western Germany. Berlin, which lies in the Soviet zone, will be occupied by all of them.

You have two goals. First, you want Stalin to join the new United Nations. Second, you want the Soviets to join the fight against Japan. Military experts think Japan could hold out for 18 months after Germany falls unless the Soviets join the war in Asia.

Stalin has his own goals. He wants to control eastern and central Europe. The Red Army already occupies Poland. You and Churchill urge Stalin to let Poland become a self-governing democracy. Stalin refuses. He says that control of Poland matters more to him than taking part in the United Nations. He will join the war against Japan, but he demands territory from Japan and China.

What really happened

Roosevelt chose B. Stalin agreed to join the war against Japan and signed the Yalta Declaration of Liberated Europe. It called for free and open elections in the liberated nations of eastern Europe.

But Stalin broke these promises. The Red Army “liberated” Poland and other nations in eastern Europe. It sent anything of economic value back to the Soviet Union. The Soviets imprisoned people who questioned the new Communist governments.

Critics said Roosevelt “gave away” eastern Europe. Norton says the United States could not have forced the Soviet army out of eastern Europe and had no leverage. Roosevelt said Yalta was the best he could do for Poland at that time.

Source: Norton Ch. 24, “War Aims and Strategy,” The Yalta Conference (1945), pp. 1085-1086.
