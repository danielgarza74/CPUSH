Dilemma 1 of 5 · 1930

June 1930: Sign the Tariff Bill or Veto It?

You are there

It is June 1930. You are President Herbert Hoover. The stock market crashed in October 1929. Farmers have been struggling since crop prices collapsed at the end of World War I.

Like most Republican presidents, you have supported higher tariffs. A tariff is a tax on goods from other countries. It keeps foreign products from competing with American producers.

Congress has sent you the Smoot-Hawley Tariff bill. Two Republicans, Reed Owen Smoot and Willis C. Hawley, wrote it to help farmers by raising tariffs on farm imports. Corporate lobbyists got Congress to add thousands of other items. The average tariff would jump from an already high 25 percent to 50 percent.

More than 1,000 economists have asked you to veto the bill. They say it would raise prices on most raw materials and consumer products for Americans.

What really happened

In June 1930, Hoover signed the bill. Norton says it caused another steep drop in the stock market. The average tariff rate became the highest in history.

As predicted, other countries passed tariffs of their own. That made it harder for American farms and businesses to sell their products abroad. U.S. exports fell sharply, and so did trade around the world. This worsened the Depression.

The tariff also made it harder for European countries to sell goods in the United States. Those countries then had less money to buy American goods. Norton lists the tariff as one of several causes of the Great Depression.

Source: Norton Ch. 23, “The Causes of the Great Depression,” Government’s Action and the Economy, p. 1003, and The European Influence, p. 1004.

Dilemma 2 of 5 · 1935

1935: Leave the Dust Bowl or Stay?

You are there

It is 1935. You live on a farm in Oklahoma, one of the states hit hardest by the Dust Bowl. A terrible drought has hit the southern plains. Your crops have withered, and your income has dropped.

For decades, farmers in your state overfarmed the land. That robbed the soil of nutrients and made the drought worse. Strong winds scoop up millions of tons of dry topsoil. Dark dust clouds, called “black blizzards,” cover farms and towns.

Farmers cannot pay their debts, and banks are foreclosing on their property. Many families are leaving. People say jobs are plentiful in California.

What really happened

Many families chose A. During the 1930s and 1940s, some 800,000 people, mostly White, left Arkansas, Texas, Missouri, and Oklahoma for the Far West. People often called them “Okies” or “Arkies.” Norton’s photo shows one sharecropping family arriving in Bakersfield, California, in 1935, after they “got blowed out in Oklahoma.”

But California was not what many had hoped. Norton says rural California was “no paradise.” Only a few migrants could afford to buy land. Many lived in tents or crude cabins and were often on the move. They suffered from bad weather, poor sanitation, and social abuse.

One Okie said that when the big farmers “need us they call us migrants, and when we’ve picked their crop, we’re bums and we got to get out.”

Source: Norton Ch. 23, “The Human Toll of the Depression,” Dust Bowl Migrants, pp. 1008-1009.

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1932

1932: Who Should Help the Jobless?

You are there

It is 1932. The Great Depression is in its third year. Unemployment was 4 million in 1930 and 8 million in 1931. Now it is 12 million. Perhaps one-quarter of all Americans cannot afford housing or enough food.

Homeless families live in shacks made of cardboard and scrap. People call these villages “Hoovervilles.”

President Hoover will not support federal programs that give direct help to needy people. He fears the nation would be “plunged into socialism.” He says government help would rob people of the desire to help themselves. He hopes local charities, churches, and volunteers will do the job.

Congress has created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC). It lends money to banks, life insurance companies, and railroads. Senator Robert F. Wagner of New York asks why the government will not “extend a helping hand” to the American “who has been without wages since 1929.”

What really happened

In 1932, Hoover held to his view. Norton says he misjudged how deep the crisis was. Most unemployed Americans wanted jobs, but there were no jobs, even for hardworking people. Norton says there was no federal effort to address homelessness and joblessness.

In the election of 1932, Franklin Roosevelt won about 23 million votes to Hoover’s 16 million. Hoover had warned that Roosevelt’s plans for government action “would destroy the very foundations of our American system.” Norton says Hoover lost because his administration failed to address the human distress of the Depression.

After Roosevelt took office, the federal government for the first time took responsibility for helping the most desperate Americans. Norton says the number of people in need was far greater than charities and local agencies could handle.

Source: Norton Ch. 23, “From Hooverism to the New Deal,” pp. 1010-1014, and “Roosevelt’s New Deal,” p. 1018.

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1933

March 1933: Trust the Banks Again?

You are there

It is March 12, 1933. You have some savings. For years, banks have been failing. More than 9,000 banks had closed by 1932. Millions of people who kept money in those banks went bankrupt with them.

Franklin Roosevelt has been president for a little over a week. On March 5, he asked Congress to pass an emergency banking law. It declared a four-day bank holiday. For the first time, all U.S. banks closed their doors. Now the banks are reopening.

Today, Roosevelt gives his first radio “fireside chat.” About 60 million people are listening. He says it is safer to “keep your money in a reopened bank than under the mattress.”

What really happened

The next day, people took their money back to the banks. One of Roosevelt’s advisers said, “Capitalism was saved in eight days.”

On June 16, 1933, Roosevelt signed the Glass-Steagall Banking Act. It created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC). The federal government now insured customer bank accounts up to $2,500. Norton says this made future panics less likely.

Norton says that by the end of the first year, the First New Deal had propped up the banking industry. But many Americans still struggled with poverty.

Source: Norton Ch. 23, “Roosevelt’s New Deal,” Banking Regulation, pp. 1017-1018.

Dilemma 5 of 5 · 1937

February 1937: Add Justices to the Supreme Court?

You are there

It is February 1937. You are a member of Congress. President Roosevelt has just won a landslide reelection. He carried every state except Maine and Vermont.

But the Supreme Court has been striking down parts of the New Deal. In 1935, it ruled that the National Industrial Recovery Act was unconstitutional. By the end of the 1935-1936 term, it had ruled against New Deal programs in seven of nine major cases.

Roosevelt calls the Court “horse-and-buggy” and its justices “nine old men.” Their average age is seventy-one. Lawsuits against the Social Security Act and the Wagner Act are waiting before the Court. Roosevelt fears these laws could be struck down.

On February 5, Roosevelt tells reporters that he will ask Congress to add up to six new justices, one for each justice over seventy. He says the aging justices are falling behind in their work. Congress, not the Constitution, decides the size of the Court. It has had between six and ten justices. Now it has nine. Opponents call this plan “Court-packing.”

What really happened

Roosevelt would not back down. He said, “The people are with me, I know it.” Critics spoke out. The journalist Walter Lippmann said Roosevelt had become “drunk with power.” The Senate Judiciary Committee said the bill should be “so emphatically rejected” that nothing like it would ever be presented again.

On July 22, 1937, the Senate overwhelmingly voted the bill down. Norton calls the Court-packing plan the worst blunder of Roosevelt’s presidency. It damaged his prestige and split the Democratic Party. More conservative Democrats, mostly from the South, then openly opposed his plans.

Norton says that in a time of rising authoritarianism in Europe, many Americans saw a president changing the balance among the three branches of government as a dangerous break from political norms.

Source: Norton Ch. 23, “The Second New Deal,” The Court-Packing Plan, pp. 1041-1042.
