Dilemma 1 of 5 · 1803

1803: Buy Louisiana, or Say No?

You are there

It is 1803, and you are an adviser to President Thomas Jefferson. His diplomats went to Paris to buy the port of New Orleans from France. France is ruled by Napoléon Bonaparte. Then Napoléon surprised them. He offered to sell all of Louisiana, a huge area from the Mississippi River west to the Rocky Mountains.

The price is $15 million, about 3 cents an acre. Jefferson fears that if France controls the Mississippi River, the two countries will end up at war. Owning Louisiana would also keep other powers far from the United States. Many Indigenous nations have lived on this land for generations and claim it as their own.

The signed treaty has just reached Washington, D.C. But there is a problem. The Constitution does not say anything about buying territory. Was this legal? Many New England Federalists strongly oppose the purchase. They say the president has no power to buy so much land, and they fear it will weaken New England.

What really happened

Jefferson chose to buy. Norton says his wish to double the size of the republic was stronger than his worry that the purchase was unconstitutional. He said the land would promote “the peace and security of the nation in general.”

The Senate approved the treaty 26 to 6 in October 1803. In December, U.S. officials took formal possession. The purchase was 875,000 square miles.

Norton adds that France did not really own this land. France sold its colonial rights, and Americans then used those rights to take land from Indigenous people. In the years after, Indigenous nations paid a heavy price in war and in court fights to keep their homelands.

Source: Norton Ch. 7, “Jeffersonian Republicanism” (opens p. 284), “The Louisiana Purchase” (near p. 292).

Dilemma 2 of 5 · 1807

1807: Stop All Foreign Trade, or Keep Sailing?

You are there

It is late 1807, and you are an adviser to President Thomas Jefferson. Britain and France are at war. Each one tries to stop American ships from trading with the other. British warships have seized American merchant ships. French warships have done the same. Hundreds of American ships were captured in 1807.

British ships also stop American ships and take sailors off them. This is called impressment. In June, the British warship Leopard fired on the American ship Chesapeake off the coast of Virginia. Three Americans were killed and eighteen were wounded.

Many Americans want to fight back. But the U.S. Army and Navy are small and not ready for war. Jefferson says his choices are “war, embargo, or nothing.” An embargo means the government stops ships from sailing to other countries. War is not a good choice right now, so you must pick between the other two.

What really happened

Jefferson chose the embargo. He convinced enough Republicans in Congress to pass the Embargo Act in December 1807. It stopped all American exports. He called the plan “peaceable coercion.” He and Secretary of State James Madison expected that Britain and France would give in.

It did not work. Neither Britain nor France was frightened by losing trade with America. But the embargo hurt the American economy. U.S. exports fell from $48 million in 1807 to $9 million a year later. Money the government collected from tariffs fell from $18 million to $8 million. Shipbuilding dropped by two-thirds. Farmers and planters in the South and West saw prices for their exported crops cut in half.

Norton says some merchants had decided to take their chances and keep sailing. Hundreds of their ships were captured in 1807.

Source: Norton Ch. 7, “War in the Mediterranean and Europe” (begins p. 300), “The Embargo Act (1807)”.

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1812

1812: Declare War on Britain, or Do Not?

You are there

It is 1812, and you are an adviser to President James Madison. For years, Britain has stopped American ships and taken sailors off them. This is called impressment. Between 1803 and 1811, about 6,200 American sailors were forced into the British navy.

On the western frontier, Indigenous nations are fighting to stop American settlers from moving onto their land. The British support some of these Indigenous forces. Some Americans also want to take Canada from Britain. They point out that there are nearly 8 million Americans and only 300,000 Canadians.

Members of Congress called war hawks, like Henry Clay of Kentucky and John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, want war. In New England and much of New York, people do not. War would hurt the shipping business there. Also, the United States is not ready for war, either with money or with soldiers. Britain is busy fighting Napoléon in Europe.

Congress has never before declared war. What would you advise?

What really happened

Congress declared war on Britain. Norton says this was the first time Congress ever declared war. Madison and the Republicans believed that only war would end impressment and stop the attacks along the western frontier. Norton also says Britain had promised in June 1812 to quit interfering with American shipping.

The war did not go well at first. The British navy blockaded American ports, and the government’s tariff money fell. Massachusetts and Connecticut refused to send soldiers, and some merchants sold supplies to British troops in Canada.

Norton says there was no clear military winner in the War of 1812, and little was settled about the issues that started it.

Source: Norton Ch. 7, “The War of 1812” (begins p. 305), “War Fever” and “War Preparations”; “The War’s Legacies” (in the section that begins p. 316).

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1814

1814: Meet in Hartford, or Wait for Peace?

You are there

It is late 1814, and you are a Federalist leader in New England. The United States has been at war with Britain for more than two years. You and many Federalists call it “Mr. Madison’s War.” You opposed it from the start. The war is costing a lot of money.

New England Federalists also feel they have lost power. They believe Republicans, especially in the South, run the national government. Some Federalists want to hold a meeting in Hartford, Connecticut. Delegates would write changes to the Constitution to limit Republican power. They might also warn that some New England states could secede, which means leave the Union, if they are ignored.

At the same time, American diplomats, including Henry Clay and John Quincy Adams, are meeting with British officials in Ghent, in Europe. They are trying to end the war. Nobody knows yet whether they will succeed.

What really happened

The New England Federalists held the Hartford Convention a few weeks before the Battle of New Orleans. Delegates proposed amendments to limit Republican and southern power. They wanted to end the counting of enslaved people when deciding how many representatives a state gets. They wanted a two-thirds vote in Congress to declare war or admit new states. They also wanted to bar presidents from the same state from serving one after another. This was aimed at Virginia. The delegates warned that some New England states might secede if their demands were ignored.

The plan fell apart. In February 1815, messengers from the convention reached Washington, D.C. They found the capital celebrating the American victory at New Orleans. Congress and the president ignored them, and the delegates went home.

Norton says the episode was fatal to the Federalist Party, which never recovered from the shame of disloyalty. In the meantime, the Treaty of Ghent had been agreed on Christmas Eve 1814, and Norton says it saved the divided republic from possible civil war and financial ruin.

Source: Norton Ch. 7, “The Aftermath of War” (begins p. 316), “The Hartford Convention”.

1834: Strike at the Mill, or Keep Working?

You are there

It is 1834, and you are a young woman who works in a cotton mill in Lowell, Massachusetts. You grew up on a farm, and now you are one of the first factory workers in the nation. You tend a spinning machine or a loom all day. The work is boring, and it is tiring.

You are paid about $2.50 a week. Men are paid more for the same work. Now the mill owners have cut wages, and they have also made the work move faster. Living conditions are getting worse. One worker calls it “factory tyranny.”

Some workers want to go on strike. That means they stop working until the owners listen. Women in other cities, such as New York in 1825 and Baltimore in 1833, have also gone on strike. But a strike is risky. Workers could lose their pay, and the owners could find other workers.

What really happened

The Lowell women went on strike in 1834. They were protesting wage cuts and worse working and living conditions. Norton says the workers lost the strike.

Two years later, the Lowell women walked out again, this time to protest higher rents in the boardinghouses owned by the company. That time the owners backed down.

Norton also says that, over time, the owners began hiring Irish immigrants. They were so desperate for jobs that they rarely complained about the conditions. By 1850, about 40 percent of the mill workers were Irish.

Source: Norton Ch. 8, “Industrial Development” (begins p. 342), “The Lowell System” and “Protests by Lowell Women”.
