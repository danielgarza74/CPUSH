1817: Federal Money for Roads and Canals, or Leave It to the States?

You are there

It is 1817, and you are an adviser to President James Madison. The nation is at peace after the War of 1812. Many leaders want a strong national economy. They are called nationalists.

Henry Clay, a Kentucky congressman, has a plan called the American System. It joins a national bank, tariffs, and federal money for internal improvements. Internal improvements are roads, bridges, canals, and harbors. Clay wants each region of the country to get its top priority.

Congress has sent Madison a bill to pay for more construction. Madison has supported this kind of spending before. But it is his last day in office, and he must decide whether to sign the bill or veto it. A veto means he refuses to sign it.

What really happened

Madison vetoed the bill. Norton says he was worried that this kind of federal spending was not allowed by the Constitution, even though he had supported internal improvements himself.

Because of this, internal improvements stayed mostly the job of the states for the next 100 years, with a few exceptions.

1825: Back Adams or Back Jackson?

You are there

It is early 1825, and you are an adviser to Henry Clay, the Kentucky congressman. The election of 1824 has no clear winner. In the Electoral College, Andrew Jackson got 99 votes, John Quincy Adams got 84, William Crawford got 41, and Clay got 37.

No one has a majority. So the Constitution says the House of Representatives must pick the president from the top three. Clay came in fourth, so he is out. But he is a powerful congressman.

Clay and Adams do not like each other. But Adams supports most of what Clay wants: high tariffs, better transportation, and a strong national bank. Clay also expects that Adams would name him secretary of state. That office usually led to the White House.

Jackson won the most electoral votes. Many of his supporters feel he should be president. What would you advise Clay to do?

What really happened

Clay chose Adams. A deal between the two men broke the deadlock. The House elected Adams, with 13 state votes to Jackson’s 7 and Crawford’s 4.

Jackson was furious. He called Clay a “scoundrel” and the “Judas of the West.” He said Clay and Adams had made a “corrupt bargain” that had “defeated the will of the People.” Norton calls Adams’s win a controversial victory. It united his foes and crippled his presidency before it began.

Norton says the election showed how deeply divided the nation had become. In his view, sectionalism had defeated nationalism as the Republicans split into warring regional groups. Jackson came back in 1828 and easily beat Adams.

Source: Norton Ch. 9, “The Rise of Andrew Jackson” (begins p. 382), “The Election of 1824” and “The ‘Corrupt Bargain’”.

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1832

1832: Sign the Bank Bill or Veto It?

You are there

It is the summer of 1832, and you are an adviser to President Andrew Jackson. Congress has passed the Bank Recharter Bill. It would renew the Second Bank of the United States, the “B.U.S.” Congress set up the bank in 1816 for twenty years. It helps keep a stable national currency, and Norton says it benefited the national economy.

The bank has been controversial from the start. Many local banks and state governments, especially in the South and West, fear its growing power. Southerners and westerners say the huge bank holds back lending by state banks and makes it harder for businesses to borrow.

In 1819, the Supreme Court ruled in McCulloch v. Maryland that Congress has the power to create the bank. Jackson has hated banks for years. He once called bankers “vipers and thieves.”

The president must sign the bill or veto it. What would you advise?

What really happened

Jackson vetoed the bill. He said the bank was unconstitutional, even though the Supreme Court had ruled the other way. He said it was “dangerous to our liberties.” He also charged that it made “the rich richer and the potent more powerful” and was unfair to farmers, mechanics, and laborers.

Norton says the bank had benefited the economy. Norton also says Jackson wanted to make democracy and economic chances open to the “common man.” Jackson’s critics said he acted like a king. They called him “King Andrew I,” and they formed a new party called the Whigs.

Jackson’s war on the bank did not end with the veto. Norton says it led small state banks to take risky actions, the very kind of actions Jackson most feared.

Source: Norton Ch. 10, “The Bank War” and “The Recharter Effort”; Ch. 10, “The Whig Coalition”.

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1832

1832: Can a State Say No to a Federal Law?

You are there

It is November 1832, and you live in South Carolina. Many people in your state blame their money troubles on the Tariff of 1828. People call it the Tariff of Abominations. It put a tax on British cloth coming into the country. That hurt cotton growers, because Britain then bought less raw cotton from America. It also raised the price of imported goods.

John C. Calhoun, a leader from South Carolina, wrote a pamphlet in secret called the South Carolina Exposition and Protest. He said the tariff helped New England’s textile factories more than Southern farming. He said a state could “nullify,” or veto, a federal law it thinks is unconstitutional.

President Jackson has tried to calm the fight. In 1831 he asked Congress to lower the tariff, and Congress passed the lower Tariff of 1832. It was not enough for Calhoun and many others in South Carolina. President Jackson and others warn that if states pick which federal laws to obey, there will be chaos.

A state convention is meeting to decide. Which side would you back?

What really happened

The South Carolina convention overwhelmingly chose nullification. It declared the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 “null, void, and no law.” It said South Carolina would secede from the Union if the government used force to collect the tariff. Other Southern states felt sympathy, but none supported nullification. South Carolina stood alone.

Jackson answered. He promised “firmness and forbearance,” but he said nullification “means insurrection and war.” In his December 4, 1832 message he said, “Disunion by armed force is treason.” He sent soldiers and a warship to Charleston. The governor of South Carolina mobilized the state militia. Jackson asked Congress for a Force Bill, which would let him use the army to make South Carolina obey the law.

Then Henry Clay offered a plan to lower the tariff slowly. On March 1, 1833, Congress passed both the lower tariff and the Force Bill. Calhoun and the nullifiers backed down. Both sides claimed victory. Jackson had upheld the Union, and South Carolina got a lower tariff.

But Norton says a deeper problem remained. Southern slaveholders felt more and more threatened by growing anti-slavery feeling in the North.

1837: Should the Government Rescue People in a Depression?

You are there

It is the fall of 1837, and you are an adviser to President Martin Van Buren. On May 10, several big banks in New York ran out of gold and silver. They refused to turn paper money into coins. Other banks across the country quickly did the same. This is the Panic of 1837. It is the worst money crisis the young nation has faced.

The trouble began in Europe. Britain’s bank cut its loans. British companies cut their trade with America. Britain bought much less American cotton, and the price of cotton fell. Now about one-third of workers are out of a job. Those who still have jobs have lower pay, and prices for food and clothing are shooting up.

The government keeps its money in state banks, and some of those banks are in trouble. Some people say the government must step in to help hard-pressed farmers, bankers, and businesses, and also people who have lost their jobs and homes. Van Buren does not think the government has that duty. He believes the government should stop risking its money in unsafe banks.

What really happened

Van Buren held to his view. He did not believe the government had any duty to rescue anyone. His critics called him “Martin Van Ruin.”

He wanted an independent Treasury, where the government would keep its funds in its own vaults and deal only in gold or silver. It took him more than three years to get Congress to pass the Independent Treasury Act. It became law on July 4, 1840. The Whigs repealed it in 1841, and it was restored in 1846.

The Panic grew into the worst depression the country had seen, and it lasted seven years. Poor people, enslaved Black people, and Native Americans were hit especially hard. In the 1840 election, the Whigs won. William Henry Harrison beat Van Buren with 234 electoral votes to 60. Norton says the Whigs promised a return to good times without explaining how. It was simply time for a change. More than 80 percent of White men voted, the highest turnout before or since.

Source: Norton Ch. 10, “Political Battles” (page numbers not confirmed).
