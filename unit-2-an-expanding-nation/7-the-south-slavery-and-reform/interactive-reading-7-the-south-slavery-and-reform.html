Racial Capitalism and the Cotton Kingdom

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 1: Explain how racial capitalism contributed to inequalities in the South.

Core Objective 2: Discuss the role that cotton production and slavery played in the South’s economic and social development.

IN MY WORDS: How did cotton and slavery make some people in the South rich and others unequal?

Reading ARacial Capitalism

In the South, a cotton empire developed where Northern financiers, shippers, and merchants provided an industrial and financial infrastructure in which large-scale cotton production could thrive. It was in the South that a new form of capitalism found its roots: racial capitalism.

Slavery was a profit-making system built upon forced labor; and it was a form of racial capitalism that created vast inequalities in income, wealth, power, and status. These disparities were so great that the monetary value of enslaved people exceeded the total wealth that the entire nation generated from railroads and factories.

Yet most White Southerners viewed those held in bondage not as human beings but rather as property. . . . Poor Whites who owned no enslaved people, and resented those who did, could still claim racial superiority over Black people. These attitudes ensured that racial capitalism would thrive throughout the South.

Word help: financiers = people who lend money · infrastructure = the banks, ships, and systems a business needs · capitalism = an economy run for private profit · inequalities = unfair gaps · disparities = big differences · bondage = slavery · superiority = being above others

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 11, “Racial Capitalism”.

Reading B“Cotton Is King”

During the first half of the nineteenth century, cotton surpassed rice as the most profitable cash crop in the South. Southern cotton drove much of the nation’s economy and played a key role in the Industrial Revolution, feeding the textile mills in New England and Great Britain.

“Cotton is King,” exclaimed the Southern Cultivator journal in 1859, “and wields an astonishing influence over the world’s commerce.” . . . By 1860, southern states were providing 77 percent of the cotton used in Britain’s textile mills and 90 percent of the fiber used in France’s mills.

By 1860, enslaved people represented nearly half the population of the Lower South.

Word help: surpassed = passed; became bigger than · cash crop = a crop grown to sell · textile mills = factories that make cloth · wields = uses · commerce = trade · fiber = thread material

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 11, “The Cotton Kingdom” (“King Cotton”) (pp. 430–431).

Reading CMoving to the Old Southwest

The so-called cotton kingdom moved south and west during the first half of the nineteenth century. As Virginia and the Carolinas experienced soil exhaustion from the overplanting of tobacco and cotton, restless farmers and many sons of enslavers moved to the Old Southwest—western Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, Arkansas, and, eventually, Texas. They found cheap land and fertile soil—a promised land made possible by the worldwide demand for cotton clothing.

Word help: soil exhaustion = soil worn out from too much farming · restless = wanting to move · enslavers = people who held others in slavery · fertile = good for growing crops · demand = how much people want to buy

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 11, “The Cotton Kingdom” (A Fertile Cotton Kingdom) (p. 432).

Whites in the South

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 3: Explain why most White Southerners—enslavers as well as those who did not have the means to own enslaved people—supported the expansion of slavery.

IN MY WORDS: Why did most White Southerners, even poor ones who owned no one, want slavery to spread?

Reading DPlanters and the Plantation Mistress

Over time, the culture of cotton and slavery shaped the South’s social structure and provided much of its political power. Unlike in the North and the Midwest, White Southern society was dominated by an elite group of enslavers and merchants.

If, as historians have agreed, one had to own at least twenty laborers to be called a planter, only 1 out of 30 Whites in 1860 qualified.

The plantation mistress may have been expected to be meekly submissive, but few were the frail, helpless creatures that many husbands demanded. . . . These women were active participants in the system of slavery. Many mistresses doled out work assignments and punishments; many organized private and public slave auctions.

Word help: elite = a small, powerful group · dominated = controlled · planter = an owner of a large plantation · meekly submissive = quietly obeying · frail = weak · doled out = handed out · auctions = public sales

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 11, “Whites in the South” (Enslavers; The Plantation Mistress) (pp. 438–439).

Reading E“Plain White Folk”

The most numerous White Southerners were small farmers—the so-called plain White folk, usually uneducated and often illiterate, eking out hardscrabble lives of bare self-sufficiency.

Although only a minority of the small-farm owners held enslaved people, most supported slavery for economic and racial reasons. They feared that the enslaved people, if freed, would compete with them for land and jobs, and they enjoyed the privileged status that race-based slavery afforded them.

Overall, despite the sharp class differences that slavery created, most White Southerners were fiercely loyal to the institution. The planter elite and their sons, of course, wanted to expand slavery so they could establish even bigger plantations on cheap, fertile land in the Old Southwest.

Word help: illiterate = not able to read · eking out = barely making · hardscrabble = poor and hard · self-sufficiency = making just what you need · minority = less than half · privileged status = a higher place in society · institution = an established system (here, slavery)

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 11, “Whites in the South” (“Plain White Folk”; “Poor Whites”) (pp. 441–442).

Enslaved People: Life and Resistance

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 4: Describe the impact of slavery on African Americans, both free and enslaved, throughout the South.

Core Objective 5: Analyze how enslaved people resisted the conditions they were forced to endure.

IN MY WORDS: How did slavery control African Americans, and how did enslaved people fight back?

Reading FSlave Codes and “Free Persons of Color”

In 1790, the United States had fewer than 700,000 Black enslaved people. By 1830, they numbered more than 2 million—and by 1860, 4 million

Formal slave codes (restrictive legislation that sought to control Black people) were quite common. Each state governed the treatment of enslaved people: they could not leave their enslaver’s land or household without permission in the form of a pass, they had nightly curfews, and some enslavers incorporated “bed checks” to make sure the enslaved people were in their cabins by 9:00 P.M.

African Americans who were not enslaved were called “free persons of color.” In fact, however, they were anything but free; they occupied an uncertain social status between slavery and freedom.

Word help: restrictive = limiting · legislation = laws · pass = a written note giving permission · curfew = a time you must be inside · incorporated = added · status = place in society

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 11, “Enslaved People in the South” (White Supremacy and Slave Codes; “Free Persons of Color”) (pp. 442–444).

Reading GField Hands

Although many enslaved people were artisans or craftsmen (carpenters, blacksmiths, furniture-makers, butchers, ship pilots, house servants, cooks, nurses, maids, weavers, basketmakers, and so on), the vast majority were field hands who were often organized into work gangs supervised by a Black driver or a White overseer.

Field hands worked from sunrise to sunset, six days a week. Solomon Northup remembered picking cotton until it was “too dark to see, and when the moon is full, they oftentimes labor till the middle of the night.” Each evening, the overseer or planter weighed the baskets and recorded that weight next to each picker’s name on a slate board. Those who fell short of the daily goal were whipped.

Word help: artisans = skilled workers who make things · supervised = watched and directed · overseer = a boss who watched the workers · oftentimes = often · slate board = a small chalkboard

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 11, “Enslaved People in the South” (Slavery as a Way of Life) (pp. 450–451).

Reading HCommunity, Rebellion, and the Dream of Freedom

Wherever they could, they forged their own sense of community, asserted their individuality, and devised many ways of resisting their confinement. Many spirituals (sacred folk songs) expressed the longing to be free.

The Nat Turner rebellion of August 1831, in Southampton County, Virginia, again panicked Whites throughout the South. Turner, a trusted Black overseer, was also a preacher who believed God had instructed him to lead a slave rebellion.

More than any other slave uprising, Nat Turner’s Rebellion terrified Whites across the South. The Virginia legislature debated whether slavery should be abolished. That proposal was defeated, and instead the delegates restricted the ability of enslaved people to learn to read and write and gather for religious meetings.

Yet thousands of enslaved people kept running away each year—a powerful example of the enduring dream of freedom and the courage of those who yearn for it.

Word help: forged = built · asserted = showed strongly · devised = thought up · confinement = being held captive · sacred = holy · abolished = ended · delegates = lawmakers · enduring = lasting

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 11, “Forging an Enslaved Community” (Slave Rebellions; The Dream of Freedom) (pp. 453–458).

Religion and Transcendentalism

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 6: Describe the major changes in religious life in the early nineteenth century and assess their influence on American society.

Core Objective 7: Examine the emergence of transcendentalism in American culture in the early nineteenth century.

IN MY WORDS: How did religion change in the early 1800s, and what did the transcendentalists believe?

Reading IUnitarians and Universalists

A growing number of Protestant churches stressed that everyone is capable of perfection through the guiding light of Christ and their own activism.

Unitarians also stressed that people were not inherently sinful. By following the teachings of Jesus and trusting their own consciences, all people were eligible for salvation. Churches that adopted the Unitarian outlook were especially popular with the educated elite in major cities such as Boston.

A parallel anti-Calvinist movement, Universalism, attracted a different—and much larger—social group: the working poor. . . . Like the Unitarians, Universalists proclaimed the dignity and worth of all people.

Word help: Protestant = Christian churches that are not Catholic · perfection = being without fault · inherently sinful = born bad · consciences = inner sense of right and wrong · salvation = being saved by God · elite = rich, educated people · dignity = worth and respect

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 12, “A More Democratic Religion” (Unitarianism and Universalism) (pp. 464–465).

Reading JThe Second Great Awakening

Around 1800, the United States experienced a massive wave of religious revivals called the Second Great Awakening, or the Great Revival. . . . While all denominations grew, new evangelical sects—Baptists, Methodists, and Presbyterians—experienced explosive popularity. In 1780, the nation had only 50 Methodist churches; by 1860, there were 20,000, far more than any other denomination.

Both shared a simple message: Salvation is available to anyone who repents and embraces the teachings of Christ.

In its frontier phase, the Second Great Awakening generated tremendous excitement and emotional excesses. It gave birth to two religious phenomena—the traveling evangelist and the camp meeting.

The burned-over district in New York gave rise to several significant movements; among them was Mormonism. . . . Eventually, Smith formed what came to be called the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, informally known as the Mormon Church.

Word help: revivals = big, emotional religious meetings · denominations = church groups · evangelical = focused on spreading the faith and being born again · repents = is sorry for sins · evangelist = a traveling preacher · Smith = Joseph Smith Jr., the founder

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 12, “A More Democratic Religion” (The Second Great Awakening; Frontier Revivals; The Mormons) (pp. 465–470).

Reading KTranscendentalism

Transcendentalism was a vibrant philosophical and literary movement that promoted a radical individualism and personal spirituality liberated from the strict requirements of organized religion and societal institutions. . . . In their view, individuals could access spiritual truths through their own intuition rather than being told what to think by ministers.

During the 1840s, he became the nation’s most popular public speaker, crisscrossing the country encouraging self-reliance.

“I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately,” he wrote in Walden, or Life in the Woods (1854), “ . . . and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived.” . . . His disgust for the war led him to refuse to pay taxes, for which he was put in jail . . . “If the law is of such a nature that it requires you to be an agent of injustice to another,” Thoreau wrote, “then, I say, break the law.”

Word help: radical individualism = trusting yourself above everyone else · liberated = set free · intuition = inner feeling of what is true · he (paragraph 2) = Ralph Waldo Emerson · self-reliance = depending on yourself · deliberately = with careful thought · he (paragraph 3) = Henry David Thoreau · the war = the war against Mexico

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 12, “Romanticism in America” (Transcendentalism; Ralph Waldo Emerson; Henry David Thoreau) (pp. 473–476).

The Reform Impulse

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 8: Explain why major social reform movements emerged in the early nineteenth century and analyze their influence on American society and politics.

IN MY WORDS: Why did so many reform movements start in the early 1800s, and what did they try to change?

Reading LWhy Reform?

By the 1840s, the United States was awash in social reform movements led by dreamers and activists, mostly women, who fought against social injustice and perceived immorality. . . . the Second Great Awakening was not focused simply on promoting individual conversions; it was also intended to “reform human society.”

The rise of an urban middle class enabled growing numbers of women to hire cooks and maids, thus freeing them to devote more time to societal concerns.

The invigorating revivals led by Finney and others provided much of the energy behind the widespread reform impulse that swept across America during the first half of the nineteenth century.

Word help: awash = flooded · reform = change to make better · perceived immorality = what people saw as wrong behavior · conversions = people turning to religion · urban = city · invigorating = full of energy · impulse = strong push

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 12, “The Reform Impulse” (p. 480). Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 12, “A More Democratic Religion” (Charles G. Finney) (p. 469).

Reading MTemperance

The temperance crusade was among the most widespread of the reform movements. Many people argued that most social problems of the time were rooted in alcohol abuse.

In 1826, a group of ministers in Boston organized the American Society for the Promotion of Temperance, which sponsored lectures, press campaigns, and the formation of local and state societies. A favorite tactic was to ask everyone who pledged to quit drinking to put by their signature a letter T for “total abstinence.” With that, a new word entered the English language: teetotaler.

Word help: temperance = drinking less or no alcohol · crusade = a strong campaign · rooted in = caused by · sponsored = paid for · pledged = promised · abstinence = not drinking at all

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 12, “The Reform Impulse” (Temperance) (p. 480).

Reading NPublic Schools and Utopian Communities

By 1830, however, no state had a public school system.

Horace Mann of Massachusetts, a state legislator and attorney, led the early drive for statewide, tax-supported public schools. He proposed that the schools be free to all children regardless of gender, class, race, or ethnicity—including immigrant children. Universal access to education, Mann argued, would be “the great equalizer of the conditions of men—the balance-wheel of the social machinery.”

During the nineteenth century, more than 100 utopian communities were created. Religious motives animated many of them, while others reflected faith in the Enlightenment ideal that every social problem had a solution discoverable by scientific study.

Word help: tax-supported = paid for by taxes · ethnicity = cultural background · universal access = open to everyone · equalizer = something that makes people more equal · utopian = trying to be perfect · animated = drove

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 12, “The Reform Impulse” (Early Public Schools; Utopian Communities) (pp. 482–484).

Anti-Slavery and Women’s Rights

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 9: Evaluate the evolution of the anti-slavery movement in the first half of the nineteenth century and describe how it affected the cause of women’s rights.

IN MY WORDS: How did the fight against slavery change over time, and how did it lead to the fight for women’s rights?

Reading OThe Underground Railroad and Colonization

Between 1810 and 1850, tens of thousands of enslaved people in the South fled north. . . . The systems of safe houses and shelters in the border states such as Maryland and Kentucky (and farther north) were referred to as the Underground Railroad.

Harriet Tubman, the most celebrated member of the Underground Railroad, was born an enslaved person on Maryland’s Eastern Shore in 1820 but escaped to Philadelphia in 1849 . . . she would return to the South nineteen times to help 300 freedom seekers, including her parents and brothers, and “never lost a passenger.”

Such gradualism was evident in the formation of the American Colonization Society (ACS) in Washington, D.C., in 1816. Its mission was to raise funds to “repatriate” free Blacks back to Africa. . . . Most leaders of the free Black community denounced the colonization idea. The United States, they stressed, was their native land, and they had as valid a claim on U.S. citizenship as anyone else.

Word help: fled = ran away · border states = slave states next to free states · celebrated = famous · gradualism = changing slowly, step by step · repatriate = send back to a homeland · denounced = spoke strongly against · valid claim = a fair right

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 12, “Anti-Slavery and Women’s Rights Movements” (The Underground Railroad; Colonization) (pp. 487–489).

Reading PFrom Gradualism to Abolitionism

The 1830s marked a turning point. As more and more Americans began to realize that gradual approaches to ending slavery were insufficient, they started advocating for immediate abolitionism everywhere.

To the new generation of reformers who came of age amid the Second Great Awakening, slavery was a sin and Christians had an obligation to purge all sins, personal and societal.

In 1831, free Black northerners helped convince Garrison to launch an anti-slavery newspaper, The Liberator. . . . In pursuing that dream, he vowed, “I am in earnest—I will not equivocate—I will not excuse—I will not retreat a single inch—and I WILL BE HEARD.”

By 1840, some 160,000 people in more than 200 local branches belonged to the American Anti-Slavery Society . . . In 1840, activists formed the Liberty Party to elect an American president who would restrict the spread of slavery.

Word help: insufficient = not enough · advocating = arguing for · abolitionism = the movement to end slavery · obligation = duty · purge = get rid of · Garrison = William Lloyd Garrison, a White abolitionist · equivocate = avoid saying clearly what you mean

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 12, “Anti-Slavery and Women’s Rights Movements” (From Gradualism to Abolitionism; William Lloyd Garrison; The Liberty Party) (pp. 489–499).

Source QFrederick Douglass, “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?” (Primary Source)

Frederick Douglass escaped slavery in 1838 and became a famous speaker for the abolitionist movement. On July 5, 1852, one day after the nation celebrated its independence, he spoke to an audience in Rochester, New York.

“Fellow-citizens, pardon me, allow me to ask, why am I called upon to speak here to-day? . . . Are the great principles of political freedom and of natural justice, embodied in that Declaration of Independence, extended to us? . . . What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July? I answer; a day that reveals to him, more than all other days in the year, the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim. To him, your celebration is a sham; your boasted liberty, an unholy license . . . your shouts of liberty and equality, hollow mockery”

Word help: to-day = today · embodied = included · extended to = given to · gross = shocking · sham = a fake · unholy license = freedom used for evil · hollow mockery = empty words that make fun of the truth

Frederick Douglass, “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?” July 5, 1852. In Norton, Ch. 12, “Anti-Slavery and Women’s Rights Movements,” Comparing Perspectives (p. 497).

Reading RThe Cult of Domesticity and Seneca Falls

As abolition gained momentum, the issue of equal rights for women became more widely debated. Some anti-slavery activists joined Sojourner Truth in asserting that the fight for freedom from slavery required a more universal fight for the liberation of all people—including women.

In 1842, Catharine Beecher published A Treatise on Domestic Economy, which promoted the cult of domesticity. This powerful idea called upon women to accept and celebrate their role as manager of the household and nurturer of the children, separate from the man’s sphere of work outside the home . . . They could not vote or serve on juries.

In 1848, Lucretia Mott, a Philadelphia Quaker, and Elizabeth Cady Stanton of New York called a convention to gather in Stanton’s hometown of Seneca Falls, in western New York . . . Their Declaration of Rights and Sentiments proclaimed that “all men and women are created equal.” . . . The Declaration’s most controversial demand was the right to vote.

Word help: momentum = speed and strength · asserting = saying strongly · liberation = freedom · domesticity = home life · nurturer = caregiver · sphere = area of life · controversial = causing strong disagreement

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 12, “Anti-Slavery and Women’s Rights Movements” (Women’s Rights; Catharine Beecher; Seneca Falls) (pp. 493–494).
