Dilemma 1 of 5 · Early 1800s

Revival Tent or Quiet Church?

You are there

It is the early 1800s. A huge wave of religious revivals is spreading across the country. It is called the Second Great Awakening.

On the frontier, the revivals bring tremendous excitement. People gather at camp meetings to hear traveling preachers. The message is simple: salvation is available to anyone who repents and embraces the teachings of Christ.

Other Americans prefer a calmer path. Unitarians say people are not born sinful. They say that by following the teachings of Jesus and trusting their own consciences, all people can be saved. Universalists tell believers to use their own God-given reasoning.

You have to decide where to worship.

What really happened

The revivals grew very fast. Baptists, Methodists, and Presbyterians became hugely popular. In 1780, the nation had only 50 Methodist churches. By 1860, there were 20,000, far more than any other denomination.

Norton says the frontier revivals brought “tremendous excitement and emotional excesses.” Western New York had so much revival activity that it was called the burned-over district. The revivals gave energy to the reform movements that came next, and new groups such as the Mormon Church grew out of them. Churches that stressed reason also drew followers. Unitarian churches were popular with educated people in cities like Boston. Universalists attracted a much larger group of working poor.

Source: Norton Ch. 12, “A More Democratic Religion”.

Dilemma 2 of 5 · 1831

End Slavery Slowly or Right Now?

You are there

It is 1831. You live in the North. You have doubts about slavery and want to do something about it.

One path is gradual change. In 1816, the American Colonization Society was formed in Washington, D.C. It raises money to send free Black people back to Africa. James Madison, Andrew Jackson, and Daniel Webster support it.

Another path is new. William Lloyd Garrison is launching an anti-slavery newspaper called The Liberator. In the first issue, he condemns “the popular but pernicious doctrine of gradual emancipation.” He wants slavery to end immediately.

What really happened

The 1830s were a turning point. More and more Americans decided that gradual approaches were not enough, and they called for immediate abolition everywhere. They saw slavery as a sin that Christians had to remove. By 1840, about 160,000 people in more than 200 local branches belonged to the American Anti-Slavery Society.

Norton says some White Americans backed colonization because they opposed slavery, but others saw it as a way to get rid of free Black people. Most leaders of the free Black community rejected the idea. They said the United States was their native land, and they had as much right to citizenship as anyone. Of the first 500 subscribers to The Liberator, 450 were free Black people.

Meanwhile, slavery kept growing in the South. The enslaved population went from fewer than 700,000 in 1790 to more than 2 million in 1830, and to 4 million by 1860. Norton says most White Southerners were fiercely loyal to slavery.

Source: Norton Ch. 12, “Anti-Slavery and Women’s Rights Movements”.

Dilemma 3 of 5 · 1830s-1840s

Tax-Funded Public Schools: Support or Oppose?

You are there

It is the 1830s. You live in Massachusetts. Many people agree that a republic needs well-informed citizens, because the people hold the political power. But no state has a public school system yet.

State legislator Horace Mann has a plan. He wants statewide, tax-supported public schools. They would be free to all children, no matter their gender, class, race, or ethnicity. That includes immigrant children.

Mann says universal education would be “the great equalizer of the conditions of men—the balance-wheel of the social machinery.” He also says schools would teach values such as hard work and clean living.

But schools cost money, and the money would come from taxes. Do you support the plan?

What really happened

Mann led the early drive for statewide, tax-supported public schools. By the 1840s, most states in the North and Midwest had joined the public school movement.

But the schools had real problems. Norton says funds for buildings, books, and equipment were limited. Teachers were poorly paid and often poorly prepared.

Dilemma 4 of 5 · 1840

Change Hearts or Change Laws?

You are there

It is 1840. You belong to the American Anti-Slavery Society. It has about 160,000 members in more than 200 local branches. It says slaveholding is “a heinous crime in the sight of God.”

William Lloyd Garrison’s plan is “moral suasion.” That means peaceful persuasion that tells people slavery is a sin. Norton calls it a moral crusade rather than a political movement.

Now some members doubt that moral suasion will ever end slavery. They say political action would work best. In 1840, they form the Liberty Party. Its goal is to elect a president who would restrict the spread of slavery.

What really happened

The anti-slavery movement split over this question. Norton says one faction of the American Anti-Slavery Society decided that political action would be the most effective way to reach its goal, so it formed the Liberty Party in 1840.

All abolitionists faced hard opposition. Racism was widespread in the North, especially among the working poor. Abolitionist speakers faced hostile White crowds, and sometimes the clashes turned violent. Black people were barred from public places in the North, such as churches, schools, hotels, railroad stations, and cemeteries. Free African Americans helped turn the struggle into a fight against racial discrimination.

In the South, after Nat Turner’s Rebellion in 1831, the Virginia legislature debated whether slavery should be abolished. The proposal was defeated. Instead, lawmakers restricted the ability of enslaved people to learn to read and write and to gather for religious meetings.

Source: Norton Ch. 12, “Anti-Slavery and Women’s Rights Movements”.

Dilemma 5 of 5 · 1848

Seneca Falls: Sign or Stay Out?

You are there

It is 1848. You live in western New York. Women cannot vote or serve on juries. They are barred from the ministry and most other professions, and college is rarely an option.

Many people believe in the “cult of domesticity.” Catharine Beecher’s 1842 book promotes it. Women should manage the household and raise the children, separate from the man’s sphere of work outside the home.

Now Lucretia Mott and Elizabeth Cady Stanton have called a convention in Seneca Falls. It will discuss “the social, civil, and religious condition and rights of women.” People are asked to sign a Declaration of Rights and Sentiments. It is a revision of the Declaration of Independence. It says “all men and women are created equal.” Its most controversial demand is the right to vote.

Would you sign?

What really happened

Norton says the goals and strong language were too radical for many of the 300 delegates, and for most Americans. Even so, the gathering was an important first step in the campaign for women’s rights.

Thirty-two men joined the women in signing the Declaration. One of them was Frederick Douglass, who became a forceful supporter of women’s rights. As abolition gained momentum, women’s rights was debated more widely. Some anti-slavery activists, like Sojourner Truth, said the fight against slavery needed a wider fight for freedom for all people, including women. Women in the 1840s were not all of one mind about how to proceed.

Source: Norton Ch. 12, “Anti-Slavery and Women’s Rights Movements”.
