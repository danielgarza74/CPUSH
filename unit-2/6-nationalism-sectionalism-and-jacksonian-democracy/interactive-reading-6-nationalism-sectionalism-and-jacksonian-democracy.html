A New Nationalism

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 1: Analyze how the spirit of nationalism that emerged after the War of 1812 affected economic policies and judicial decisions.

Core Objective 2: Explain how Henry Clay’s American System fueled the rise of sectional conflict during this era.

IN MY WORDS: After the War of 1812, how did the national government grow stronger, and why did some regions get angry?

Reading AThe Bank, the Tariff, and Internal Improvements

In response, President Madison in 1816 convinced Congress to establish the Second Bank of the United States (B.U.S.). . . . Congress responded by passing the Tariff of 1816, which placed a 20–25 percent tax on a long list of imported goods.

This also meant that tariffs benefited some regions (the Northeast) more than others (the South), thus aggravating sectional tensions.

The final major element of economic nationalism in the first half of the nineteenth century involved federal financing of internal improvements (what today is called infrastructure), specifically the construction of roads, bridges, canals, and harbors.

Word help: nationalism = putting the whole nation first · tariff = a tax on goods from other countries · imported = brought in from other countries · aggravating = making worse · sectional = between regions (North, South, West) · infrastructure = roads, bridges, and canals

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 9, “A New Nationalism” (The Bank of the United States; A Protective Tariff; Internal Improvements) (p. 368).

Reading BThree Supreme Court Cases

The Supreme Court made two major decisions in 1819 that strengthened the power of the federal government at the expense of the states. One, Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819), stopped an effort by the New Hampshire legislature to alter the charter of Dartmouth College

The second major Supreme Court case of 1819 was Chief Justice Marshall’s most significant interpretation of the constitutional system: McCulloch v. Maryland (1819). . . . the Supreme Court . . . ruled unanimously that Congress had the authority to charter the B.U.S. and that states had no right to ban or to tax the national bank.

John Marshall’s last great decision, Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), affirmed the federal government’s supremacy in regulating interstate commerce.

Word help: at the expense of = by taking away from · legislature = state lawmakers · charter = an official paper that creates a school, bank, or company · unanimously = all judges agreed · supremacy = highest power · interstate commerce = trade between states

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 9, “A New Nationalism” (Protecting Contract Rights; Protecting a National Currency; Interstate Commerce).

Reading CClay’s American System Divides the Regions

The major economic initiatives debated by Congress after the War of 1812—the national bank; federal tariffs; and federally financed roads, bridges, and canals—were interrelated pieces of a comprehensive economic plan called the American System.

Critics argued that higher prices for federal lands would discourage western migration and that tariffs benefited the northern manufacturing sector at the expense of southern and western farmers and the “common” people, who had to pay higher prices for the goods produced by tariff-protected industries.

Word help: initiatives = plans · interrelated = connected · comprehensive = complete · critics = people who say what is wrong · migration = moving · manufacturing sector = factories

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 9, “Debates over the American System” (Sectional Conflicts) (p. 372).

Good Feelings Fade; the Monroe Doctrine

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 3: Identify the social and economic factors that led to the demise of the “Era of Good Feelings.”

Core Objective 4: Explain how the federal government’s diplomatic accomplishments during James Monroe’s presidency reflected the policies expressed in the Monroe Doctrine.

IN MY WORDS: What two problems ended the “Era of Good Feelings,” and what did the Monroe Doctrine tell Europe?

Reading DPanic and the Missouri Compromise

Two crucial events warned of stormy times ahead: the financial Panic of 1819 and the political conflict over statehood for the Missouri Territory.

The Panic of 1819 resulted from the sudden collapse of cotton prices . . . As the price of cotton fell and the flow of commerce slowed, banks failed, businesses went bankrupt, and unemployment soared.

The Senate decided to link Maine’s request for statehood with Missouri’s, voting to admit Maine as a free state and Missouri as a slave state, thus maintaining the political balance between free and slave states. . . . Slavery thus would continue in the Arkansas Territory and in Missouri but would be excluded from the remainder of the area west of the Mississippi River.

Word help: panic = a sudden money crisis · collapse = fall apart · bankrupt = out of money and unable to pay debts · unemployment = not having a job · balance = equal numbers · excluded = kept out

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 9, ““An Era of Good Feelings”” (James Monroe; The Panic of 1819; The Missouri Compromise) (p. 373).

Reading EA Transcontinental Nation and the Monroe Doctrine

In 1819, Adams convinced the Spanish to sign the Transcontinental Treaty (also called the Adams-Onís Treaty), which gave all of Florida to the United States in exchange for $5 million. . . . The United States now spanned the continent

The Monroe Doctrine, as it was named a generation later, contained four major points: (1) that “the American continents . . . are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers”; (2) that the United States would oppose any attempt by European nations to impose their political system anywhere in the hemisphere

Word help: treaty = an agreement between nations · spanned = stretched across · continent = the large landmass of North America · henceforth = from now on · colonization = taking land to make colonies · hemisphere = half of the world (here, North and South America)

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 9, “Nationalist Diplomacy” (A Transcontinental Nation; The Monroe Doctrine) (p. 379).

Jackson and the “Common Man”

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 5: Evaluate the social and political factors that contributed to Andrew Jackson’s success in the 1828 election.

Core Objective 6: Describe how democracy expanded and contracted during this period.

IN MY WORDS: Why did Andrew Jackson win in 1828, and who was left out of the new democracy?

Reading FThe “Corrupt Bargain”

The initial results of the 1824 election were inconclusive. In the Electoral College, Jackson received 99 votes; Adams, 84; Crawford, 41; and Clay, 37. Jackson, however, did not have the necessary majority of the votes.

A deal between Clay and Adams broke the deadlock. Clay endorsed Adams, and the House of Representatives elected Adams . . . Jackson dismissed Clay as a “scoundrel,” the “Judas of the West,” who had entered into a self-serving “corrupt bargain” with Adams. Their “corruptions and intrigues,” he charged, had “defeated the will of the People.”

Word help: inconclusive = with no clear winner · majority = more than half · deadlock = a tie that no one can break · endorsed = supported · scoundrel = a dishonest person · Judas = a traitor · intrigues = secret plans

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 9, “The Rise of Andrew Jackson” (The Election of 1824; The “Corrupt Bargain”) (p. 382).

Reading GThe Campaign of 1828 and Who Could Vote

The defiant congressional opposition to the Adams presidency launched the campaign of 1828 between the National Republicans and the Jacksonian Democrats . . . Jackson’s 1828 campaign explicitly appealed to the “common” voters, many of whom were able to vote in a presidential election for the first time.

States such as New York, in its 1821 constitution, instituted new voting restrictions on Black voting at the same time that it extended the right to vote to all White men.

Jacksonian Democrats also showed little concern for the undemocratic constraints on African Americans, Indigenous Americans, and women, all of whom were denied political and civil rights.

Word help: defiant = refusing to go along · explicitly = clearly · appealed to = tried to win over · restrictions = limits · constraints = limits · civil rights = basic rights like voting and fair treatment

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 9, “The Rise of Andrew Jackson” (The Election of 1828; The “Common Man” in Politics). Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 10, “Democratization and the Role of the Federal Government” (Anti-Democratic Impulses).

Reading HThe Spoils System

During Jackson’s two presidential terms, he replaced about one-fifth of the federal officeholders with his friends and supporters, not all of whom were qualified for their new positions. Such partisan behavior came to be called “the spoils system,” since, as a prominent Democrat declared, “to the victor goes the spoils.”

In 1831, he convinced the Democrats to stage their first presidential nominating convention as a means of involving more people in the process of selecting a candidate.

Word help: federal officeholders = people with government jobs · qualified = having the skills needed · partisan = favoring one political party · spoils = prizes taken by the winner · nominating convention = a party meeting that picks a candidate

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 10, “Democratization and the Role of the Federal Government” (The Spoils System and Presidential Conventions).

The Bank War and Nullification

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 7: Evaluate Jackson’s response to the nullification crisis.

IN MY WORDS: What did South Carolina try to do, and how did Jackson answer?

Reading IThe Bank War

Early in the summer of 1832, both houses of Congress passed the Bank Recharter Bill. A month later, however, Jackson vetoed the bill. The president claimed that the B.U.S. was both unconstitutional (although the Supreme Court disagreed) and “dangerous to our liberties.” The B.U.S. made “the rich richer and the potent more powerful,” he charged, while discriminating against “the humble members of society—the farmers, mechanics, and laborers.”

Word help: recharter = renew the bank’s permission to operate · vetoed = rejected so it did not become law · potent = powerful · discriminating against = treating unfairly · mechanics = skilled workers

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 10, “Democratization and the Role of the Federal Government” (The Bank War; The Recharter Effort).

Reading JThe Tariff of Abominations and Nullification

Most South Carolinians blamed their economic woes on the Tariff of 1828, which was labeled the Tariff of Abominations.

Under such circumstances, he argued, a state could “nullify,” or veto, a federal law it deemed unconstitutional. . . . As President Jackson and others pointed out, however, allowing states to pick and choose which federal laws they wanted to follow would create chaos.

When the South Carolina convention nullified two tariffs, Jackson had determinedly pushed through legislation that permitted the army to force the state to comply with the tariffs (the Force Bill). Congress passed both the Force Bill and a compromise tariff that satisfied both sides.

Word help: woes = troubles · abominations = hateful things · nullify = cancel; refuse to obey · deemed = judged · chaos = total disorder · comply = obey

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 10, “Nullification” (Calhoun and the Tariff; Clay’s Compromise).

Source KJackson Warns South Carolina (Primary Source)

On December 4, 1832, President Andrew Jackson sent his yearly message to the nation. He spoke to the people of South Carolina, who were following leaders like John C. Calhoun.

“The laws of the United States must be executed. . . . Those who told you that you might peaceably prevent their execution, deceived you. . . . Their object is disunion. . . . Disunion by armed force is treason.”

Word help: executed = carried out; obeyed · peaceably = peacefully · deceived = lied to · object = goal · disunion = breaking up the United States · treason = betraying your country

Andrew Jackson, annual message, December 4, 1832. In Norton, Ch. 10, “Nullification” (Jackson’s Firm Response).

Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears

CORE OBJECTIVE

Core Objective 8: Analyze Jackson’s legacy regarding the status of Indigenous people in American society.

IN MY WORDS: What did Jackson’s government do to Indigenous nations in the East?

Reading LThe Indian Removal Act

Jackson claimed that relocating the eastern Indigenous people was an act of mercy, a “wise and humane policy” that would save them from “utter annihilation.” However, this was not the perspective of the Indigenous peoples. This was their land, they maintained, and it had been for centuries prior to European settlement.

This legislation authorized Jackson to ignore previous treaty commitments and force the nearly 100,000 Indigenous people remaining in the East and South to move to federal lands west of the Mississippi River . . . In late May 1830, the Senate passed the Indian Removal Act by a slim margin. The Cherokee responded by announcing that “we see nothing but ruin before us.”

Word help: relocating = moving to a new place · humane = kind · annihilation = being destroyed completely · perspective = point of view · authorized = gave permission · treaty commitments = promises made in treaties · slim margin = by only a few votes

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 10, “Jackson’s Policy on Indigenous Americans” (Removal of Indigenous Americans).

Reading MWorcester v. Georgia and the Trail of Tears

The anti-Cherokee laws passed by the Georgia legislature, he declared, had violated “the Constitution, laws, and treaties of the United States.” He added that the Cherokee Nation was “a distinct political community” within which Georgia law had no force.

Jackson, however, forced the Cherokee and other Indigenous nations to either abide by discriminatory new state laws or relocate to federal lands west of the Mississippi River.

By 1838, when Martin Van Buren was president, 17,000 Cherokee were evicted and forcibly moved west under military guard on the Trail of Tears, an 800-mile forced removal to ceded territory in present-day Oklahoma . . . Thousands of Indigenous people, including John Ross’s wife, died along the way from exposure, disease, or starvation.

Word help: he = Chief Justice John Marshall · violated = broke · distinct = separate · abide by = obey · discriminatory = unfair to one group · evicted = forced out · exposure = harm from cold and weather · John Ross = the Cherokee leader

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 10, “Jackson’s Policy on Indigenous Americans” (Challenges in the Courts; The Trail of Tears).

Panic, Whigs, and Jackson’s Legacy

CORE OBJECTIVES

Core Objective 9: Explain the causes of the economic depression of the late 1830s and the emergence of the Whig Party.

Core Objective 10: Assess the strengths and weaknesses of Jackson’s presidency.

IN MY WORDS: What caused the hard times of 1837, and why did a new party form against Jackson?

Reading NThe Whigs and the Panic of 1837

President Jackson’s war on the B.U.S. led his opponents to create a new anti-Jackson political party. They claimed that he was ruling like a monarch, sarcastically dubbed him “King Andrew I,” . . . The new anti-Jackson coalition called itself the Whig Party . . . For the next twenty years, the Whigs and the Democrats would be the two major political parties, and for a second time a two-party system emerged.

Two key developments, the passage of the Distribution Act and the Specie Circular, together played havoc with the economy and devastated the nation’s financial system. . . . This financial crisis, the worst yet faced by the young nation, would become known as the Panic of 1837. It would soon mushroom into the republic’s worst depression, lasting seven years.

By the fall of 1837, one-third of workers were jobless, and those still fortunate enough to have jobs had their wages slashed. . . . It took Van Buren more than three years to convince Congress to pass the Independent Treasury Act on July 4, 1840.

Word help: monarch = king · sarcastically = in a mocking way · coalition = groups joined together · havoc = great damage · devastated = badly hurt · depression = a long time of hard economic times · slashed = cut sharply

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 10, “Political Battles” (The Whig Coalition; The Money Question; The Panic of 1837; An Independent Treasury).

Reading OA Messy Legacy

Yet Jackson was a controversial figure, too. He left a messy, even contradictory, legacy. He championed opportunities for the “common man” to play a greater role in the political arena. He helped establish the modern Democratic Party . . . He also saved the Union by suppressing the nullification crisis.

Jackson’s concept of “the people,” however, was limited to a democracy of White men . . . Jackson was so convinced of the rightness of his ideals that he was willing to defy constitutional limits on his authority.

Word help: controversial = causing strong disagreement · contradictory = having parts that go against each other · legacy = what someone leaves behind · championed = fought for · suppressing = stopping · defy = refuse to obey

Norton, America: The Essential Learning Edition, Ch. 10, “Beyond Jackson’s Legacy: A New Era” (A New Political Landscape).
